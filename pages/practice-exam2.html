Problem 1: Concentric-Tube Heat Exchanger (42 pts)

Consider a counterflow heat exchanger with concentric pipes. Water enters on the inside with a temperature of 20°C and leaves at 50°C. Oil enters at 100°C. The inner pipe has a diameter of 4 cm. The wall between the two pipes is 0.25 cm thick aluminum ($k = 200$ W/m·K). The diameter of the outer pipe is 9 cm. Assume constant surface temperature for Nusselt correlations. For all parts, solve symbolically and numerically. For each part you may assume variables solved in previous parts are known. If using turbulent Nusselt correlations, use $n = 0.4$.

Given for part (c): laminar, fully developed annulus with the outer surface insulated, $Nu_i = h_iD_h/k$ and $D_h = D_o - D_i$:

$D_i/D_o$00.050.100.250.50≈1.00
$Nu_i$—17.4611.567.375.744.86
Before you start: name the three diameters $D_1 = 0.04$ m (inner pipe ID, water side), $D_2 = D_1 + 2t = 0.045$ m (inner pipe OD, oil side), $D_3 = 0.09$ m (outer pipe ID). Writing these down first prevents the most common error on this problem. Note that $D_2/D_3 = 0.50$ lands exactly on a table entry, which confirms the geometry.

(a) 5 pts. What is the exit temperature of the oil?

Hint
All four water numbers are known. Whatever heat the water gains, the oil loses.
Show solution (a)

Approach

Energy balance on both streams (sheet p. 10). Steady state, insulated outside, so no heat is lost to the surroundings.

Symbolic

$$q = \dot m_wc_{p,w}(T_{w,o} - T_{w,i}) = \dot m_{oil}c_{p,oil}(T_{oil,i} - T_{oil,o})\ \Rightarrow\ T_{oil,o} = T_{oil,i} - \frac{\dot m_wc_{p,w}(T_{w,o} - T_{w,i})}{\dot m_{oil}c_{p,oil}}$$

Numbers

$$q = 0.5(4180)(50 - 20) = 62{,}700\ \text{W},\qquad T_{oil,o} = 100 - \frac{62{,}700}{1.0(2000)} = 100 - 31.35$$
$T_{oil,o} = 68.65^\circ$C ≈ 68.7°C (with $q = 62.7$ kW, which you'll reuse in part d)
$C_{oil} = 2000$ W/K is smaller than $C_w = 2090$ W/K, so the oil should change temperature more (31.4 K vs. 30 K). ✓ The oil leaves above the water's inlet temperature, as it must.

(b) 8 pts. Determine the heat transfer coefficient of the water inside the pipe. Assume the flow is fully developed.

Hint
Find $Re_D$ first. It decides between $Nu = 3.66$ and Dittus–Boelter. You're given mass flow, not velocity.
Show solution (b)

Approach

Circular tube, so $Re_D = 4\dot m/(\pi D\mu)$ (from $\dot m = \rho u_m\pi D^2/4$). Check the regime, then use the matching fully developed correlation (sheet p. 8).

Regime

$$Re_D = \frac{4\dot m_w}{\pi D_1\mu_w} = \frac{4(0.5)}{\pi(0.04)(7.5\times10^{-4})} = 21{,}200\ \gt 2300\ \Rightarrow\ \text{turbulent}$$

It's also above $10^4$, so Dittus–Boelter is in its range. Use $n = 0.4$ as the problem says (the water is being heated anyway).

Symbolic

$$h_w = \frac{k_w}{D_1}\,0.023\left(\frac{4\dot m_w}{\pi D_1\mu_w}\right)^{4/5}Pr_w^{0.4}$$

Numbers

$$Nu_D = 0.023(21{,}221)^{0.8}(5.06)^{0.4} = 0.023(2893)(1.913) = 127.3,\qquad h_w = \frac{127.3(0.62)}{0.04}$$
$Re_D = 21{,}200$ (turbulent), $Nu_D = 127$, $h_w \approx 1970$ W/m²·K
Forced convection of a liquid: 100–20,000 W/m²·K. ✓

(c) 9 pts. Determine the heat transfer coefficient of the oil in the outside pipe. Assume the flow is fully developed and the pipe is insulated on the outside. If the flow is laminar, use the correlation above. If it is turbulent, use the correlations for flow in a pipe.

Hint
Use $D_h = D_3 - D_2$ and $A_c = \frac{\pi}{4}(D_3^2 - D_2^2)$ (sheet p. 10). The shortcut $4\dot m/(\pi D\mu)$ only works for a circular cross-section, so build $Re$ from $u_m = \dot m/(\rho A_c)$.
Show solution (c)

Geometry

$$D_h = D_3 - D_2 = 0.09 - 0.045 = 0.045\ \text{m}$$ $$A_c = \frac{\pi}{4}(D_3^2 - D_2^2) = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.0081 - 0.002025) = 4.771\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$$

Regime

$$u_m = \frac{\dot m_{oil}}{\rho_{oil}A_c} = \frac{1.0}{850(4.771\times10^{-3})} = 0.247\ \text{m/s}$$ $$Re_{D_h} = \frac{\rho_{oil}u_mD_h}{\mu_{oil}} = \frac{\dot m_{oil}D_h}{A_c\mu_{oil}} = \frac{1.0(0.045)}{(4.771\times10^{-3})(0.04)} = 236$$

$236 \lt 2300$: laminar. So use the annulus table with $D_i/D_o = D_2/D_3 = 0.045/0.09 = 0.50$, giving $Nu_i = 5.74$.

Symbolic

$$h_{oil} = \frac{Nu_i\,k_{oil}}{D_3 - D_2},\qquad Nu_i = f\!\left(\frac{D_2}{D_3}\right)\ \text{from the table}$$

Numbers

$$h_{oil} = \frac{5.74(0.14)}{0.045}$$
$Re_{D_h} = 236$ (laminar), $Nu_i = 5.74$, $h_{oil} \approx 17.9$ W/m²·K
Using $D_i = 4$ cm (forgetting the wall) gives $D_h = 5$ cm and $D_i/D_o = 0.44$, which isn't in the table. Using $4\dot m/(\pi D_h\mu)$ gives $Re = 707$, which is wrong because the annulus isn't a circle. Both are still laminar here, but the method loses points.
$h_{oil}$ is about 110 times smaller than $h_w$. Expect the oil side to dominate $1/UA$ in part (d).

(d) 13 pts. What is the length $L$ of the heat exchanger? Assume a fouling resistance on the outside pipe of $R''_o = 0.0001$ m²·K/W but no fouling on the inside pipe. (Hint: solve for $UA$.) Full points for symbolic, 1 pt extra credit for numerical.

Hint
Two routes to $UA$ have to agree: $UA = q/\Delta T_{lm}$ from the temperatures, and $1/UA$ = the sum of resistances. Every resistance has $L$ in its denominator, so pull out $1/L$ and solve.
Show solution (d)

Step 1: LMTD for counterflow

At the end where oil enters, water leaves; at the other end, oil leaves and water enters.

$$\Delta T_1 = T_{oil,i} - T_{w,o} = 100 - 50 = 50\ \text{K},\qquad \Delta T_2 = T_{oil,o} - T_{w,i} = 68.65 - 20 = 48.65\ \text{K}$$ $$\Delta T_{lm} = \frac{\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2}{\ln(\Delta T_1/\Delta T_2)} = \frac{1.35}{\ln(50/48.65)} = 49.32\ \text{K}$$

Step 2: $UA$ from the rate equation

$$UA = \frac{q}{\Delta T_{lm}} = \frac{62{,}700}{49.32} = 1271\ \text{W/K}$$

Step 3: write $1/UA$ for this geometry (sheet p. 10, concentric tube)

Water touches $A_w = \pi D_1L$. The oil and the fouling layer are on the outside of the inner pipe, $A_{oil} = \pi D_2L$. The wall is a cylinder from $D_1$ to $D_2$.

$$\frac{1}{UA} = \frac{1}{h_w\pi D_1L} + \frac{\ln(D_2/D_1)}{2\pi k_{Al}L} + \frac{R''_o}{\pi D_2L} + \frac{1}{h_{oil}\pi D_2L}$$

Step 4: solve for $L$ (the symbolic answer)

$$L = \frac{q}{\Delta T_{lm}}\left[\frac{1}{h_w\pi D_1} + \frac{\ln(D_2/D_1)}{2\pi k_{Al}} + \frac{R''_o}{\pi D_2} + \frac{1}{h_{oil}\pi D_2}\right]$$

with $q$ from (a), $\Delta T_{lm}$ from step 1, $h_w$ from (b) and $h_{oil}$ from (c).

Numbers: resistance per unit length (m·K/W)

TermExpressionValueShare
Water convection$1/(h_w\pi D_1)$$4.03\times10^{-3}$1.0%
Aluminum wall$\ln(1.125)/(2\pi\cdot200)$$9.37\times10^{-5}$0.02%
Fouling (oil side)$R''_o/(\pi D_2)$$7.07\times10^{-4}$0.2%
Oil convection$1/(h_{oil}\pi D_2)$$0.396$98.8%
Sum$0.401$
$$L = 1271(0.401)$$
$L \approx 510$ m ($\Delta T_{lm} = 49.3$ K, $UA = 1271$ W/K)
510 m is long, and the table shows why: the laminar oil carries 99% of the resistance. Removing the fouling or changing the wall material would barely matter. To shorten the exchanger you'd improve the oil side (fins, or a geometry that makes the oil turbulent). Saying this in one sentence is a cheap way to show understanding.
The fouling term divides by the oil-side area ($\pi D_2L$), not the water side. The wall uses $\ln(D_2/D_1)$, and the ratio of radii equals the ratio of diameters. Because $\Delta T_1 \approx \Delta T_2$ here, the LMTD is nearly the arithmetic mean, but write the LMTD formula anyway.

(e) 7 pts. We assumed the flow is fully developed. Determine the hydrodynamic and thermal entrance lengths ($x_{fd}$ and $x_{fd,t}$) for the water and the oil.

Hint
Laminar and turbulent use different formulas (sheet p. 8). For the annulus, $D_h$ replaces $D$. Then compare each length with the $L$ you found in (d).
Show solution (e)

Water (turbulent)

$$x_{fd} \approx x_{fd,t} \approx 10D_1 = 10(0.04) = 0.4\ \text{m}$$

Oil (laminar, $Re_{D_h} = 236$, $Pr = 571$)

$$x_{fd} = 0.05Re_{D_h}D_h = 0.05(236)(0.045) = 0.53\ \text{m}$$ $$x_{fd,t} = 0.05Re_{D_h}Pr\,D_h = 0.53(571) = 303\ \text{m}$$
Water: $x_{fd} \approx x_{fd,t} \approx 0.4$ m. Oil: $x_{fd} \approx 0.53$ m, $x_{fd,t} \approx 303$ m.
Compare with $L \approx 510$ m. The water and the oil's velocity profile are fully developed almost immediately. ✓ But the oil's thermal entry length is about 60% of the exchanger, so "fully developed" is not a good assumption for the oil. In the entry region $h$ is higher than the fully developed value, so the real exchanger would be shorter than 510 m: the estimate is conservative. The question is fishing for exactly this comparison.

Problem 2: Balanced Counterflow Exchanger (15 pts)

Consider a counterflow heat exchanger with $UA = 1000$ W/K. Water flows in both streams with a mass flow rate of 0.1 kg/s and $c_p = 4180$ J/kg·K. On the cold side the water enters at 10°C. On the hot side it enters at 70°C.

Tests: ε–NTU rating (outlets unknown, so not LMTD) and the $C_r = 1$ special case. Sheet p. 10.

(a) 8 pts. What is the effectiveness of the system?

Hint
Compute $C_h$ and $C_c$. When they're equal, the general counterflow ε formula turns into 0/0. Use its limit, or think about what happens to $T_h - T_c$ along the exchanger.
Show solution (a)

Capacity rates and NTU

$$C_h = C_c = \dot mc_p = 0.1(4180) = 418\ \text{W/K}\ \Rightarrow\ C_{min} = C_{max} = 418,\quad C_r = 1$$ $$NTU = \frac{UA}{C_{min}} = \frac{1000}{418} = 2.39$$

Effectiveness for counterflow with $C_r = 1$

$$\varepsilon = \frac{NTU}{1 + NTU} = \frac{2.39}{3.39}$$

Why (if the table isn't given)

With equal capacity rates, both streams change temperature by the same amount, so $\Delta T = T_h - T_c$ is the same everywhere. At the hot end, $\Delta T = T_{h,i} - T_{c,o}$. Then $q = UA\,\Delta T = C(T_{c,o} - T_{c,i})$. Solving:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{q}{C(T_{h,i} - T_{c,i})} = \frac{UA}{C + UA} = \frac{NTU}{1 + NTU}$$
$NTU = 2.39$, $C_r = 1$, $\varepsilon \approx 0.705$
Plugging $C_r = 1$ into $\varepsilon = \dfrac{1 - e^{-NTU(1 - C_r)}}{1 - C_re^{-NTU(1 - C_r)}}$ gives 0/0. Don't stop there or approximate with $C_r = 0.99$; use the limit.

(b) 7 pts. What are the exit temperatures of the two streams?

Hint
$q = \varepsilon q_{max}$, then an energy balance on each stream.
Show solution (b)

Symbolic

$$q = \varepsilon C_{min}(T_{h,i} - T_{c,i}),\qquad T_{c,o} = T_{c,i} + \frac{q}{C_c},\qquad T_{h,o} = T_{h,i} - \frac{q}{C_h}$$

Numbers

$$q = 0.705(418)(70 - 10) = 17{,}690\ \text{W},\qquad \frac{q}{C} = \frac{17{,}690}{418} = 42.3\ \text{K}$$
$T_{c,o} \approx 52.3^\circ$C, $T_{h,o} \approx 27.7^\circ$C ($q \approx 17.7$ kW)
$\Delta T$ at the hot end is $70 - 52.3 = 17.7$ K and at the cold end $27.7 - 10 = 17.7$ K: constant, as it should be for $C_r = 1$. And $q = UA\,\Delta T = 1000(17.7) = 17.7$ kW. ✓ The cold outlet is hotter than the hot outlet, which is fine (only possible in counterflow).

Problem 3: Heated Plate, Two Orientations (25 pts)

Air at $T_\infty = 300$ K blows over a hot flat plate at 5 m/s. The plate is 4 m by 2 m. There is energy generation within the plate of 6000 W/m³, and the plate is 0.1 m thick. The plate is insulated on the bottom, so heat is only lost by convection from the top. Assume the plate surface is at a constant temperature.

For the air: $k = 0.03$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.7$, $\mu = 2.08\times10^{-5}$ N·s/m², $\rho = 0.995$ kg/m³. Assume $Re_{x,c} = 5\times10^5$.

Tests: laminar vs. mixed flat-plate correlations (sheet pp. 5–6), a surface energy balance with generation, and how $\bar h$ scales with plate length.

Setup that covers both (a) and (b) Steady state with an insulated bottom: everything generated leaves through the top by convection. $$\dot q(LWt) = \bar h(LW)(T_s - T_\infty)\ \Rightarrow\ T_s = T_\infty + \frac{\dot qt}{\bar h},\qquad q'' = \dot qt = 6000(0.1) = 600\ \text{W/m}^2$$ The area and $q''$ are the same in both orientations (total $q = 4.8$ kW). Only $\bar h$ changes, because $L$ in $Re_L$ and $Nu_L$ is the length along the flow.

(a) 10 pts. What is the surface temperature if the long side is aligned with the wind ($L = 4$ m)?

Hint
Compute $Re_L$ and compare with $5\times10^5$. Above it, the boundary layer is laminar at the front and turbulent at the back: that's the "$-871$" correlation.
Show solution (a)

Regime

$$Re_L = \frac{\rho u_\infty L}{\mu} = \frac{0.995(5)(4)}{2.08\times10^{-5}} = 9.57\times10^5\ \gt 5\times10^5\ \Rightarrow\ \text{mixed}$$

Transition happens at $x_c = Re_{x,c}\mu/(\rho u_\infty) = 2.09$ m, so roughly the back half of the plate is turbulent.

Symbolic

$$T_s = T_\infty + \frac{\dot qt\,L}{k\left(0.037Re_L^{4/5} - 871\right)Pr^{1/3}}$$

Numbers

$$\overline{Nu}_L = \left[0.037(9.57\times10^5)^{0.8} - 871\right](0.7)^{1/3} = (2253 - 871)(0.888) = 1227$$ $$\bar h = \frac{1227(0.03)}{4} = 9.21\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K},\qquad T_s = 300 + \frac{600}{9.21} = 300 + 65.2$$
$Re_L = 9.57\times10^5$ (mixed), $\bar h \approx 9.2$ W/m²·K, $T_s \approx 365$ K (92°C)

(b) 10 pts. What is the surface temperature if the short side is aligned with the wind ($L = 2$ m)?

Hint
Same steps, new $Re_L$. Look closely at how it compares with $5\times10^5$.
Show solution (b)

Regime

$$Re_L = \frac{0.995(5)(2)}{2.08\times10^{-5}} = 4.78\times10^5\ \lt 5\times10^5\ \Rightarrow\ \text{laminar over the whole plate}$$

Symbolic

$$T_s = T_\infty + \frac{\dot qt\,L}{k\left(0.664Re_L^{1/2}Pr^{1/3}\right)}$$

Numbers

$$\overline{Nu}_L = 0.664(4.78\times10^5)^{1/2}(0.7)^{1/3} = 0.664(691.6)(0.888) = 408$$ $$\bar h = \frac{408(0.03)}{2} = 6.12\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K},\qquad T_s = 300 + \frac{600}{6.12} = 300 + 98.1$$
$Re_L = 4.78\times10^5$ (laminar), $\bar h \approx 6.1$ W/m²·K, $T_s \approx 398$ K (125°C)
$4.78\times10^5$ is close to $5\times10^5$, but it's below it, so the plate is laminar. Using the mixed correlation here would give $\overline{Nu} = 376$: the wrong correlation and a wrong answer.
The long orientation runs about 33 K cooler. It wins only because it's long enough to become turbulent over its back half, where $h$ is much higher. Film temperatures are $(365 + 300)/2 \approx 333$ K and $(398 + 300)/2 \approx 349$ K; the given properties (air at about 350 K) are a reasonable fit, and since they're given, no iteration is needed.

(c) 5 pts. Suppose a trip wire at the front of the plate makes the flow turbulent over the whole plate. Which orientation would you expect to have the higher average heat transfer coefficient (and lower surface temperature), and why?

Hint
Write $\bar h$ from the fully turbulent correlation and see how it depends on $L$ alone.
Show solution (c)

Scaling argument

$$\bar h = \frac{k}{L}\,0.037\left(\frac{\rho u_\infty L}{\mu}\right)^{4/5}Pr^{1/3} \propto \frac{L^{4/5}}{L} = L^{-1/5}$$

So the shorter flow length gives the higher $\bar h$: $\bar h_{2\text{ m}}/\bar h_{4\text{ m}} = 2^{1/5} = 1.15$.

Physical reason

The boundary layer thickens with distance from the leading edge, and a thicker layer means a smaller local $h$ ($h_x \propto x^{-1/5}$ when turbulent). A plate that's short in the flow direction never grows a thick boundary layer, so its average $h$ is higher.

Numbers (not required)

$L = 2$ m: $\bar h = 17.2$ W/m²·K, $T_s = 335$ K. $L = 4$ m: $\bar h = 15.0$ W/m²·K, $T_s = 340$ K.

Short side with the wind ($L = 2$ m): about 15% higher $\bar h$ and a lower $T_s$, because $\bar h \propto L^{-1/5}$ when the boundary layer is turbulent everywhere
This reverses (a)–(b). Without the trip wire the long side won only because it reached transition. Once both are fully turbulent (or both fully laminar, $\bar h \propto L^{-1/2}$), shorter always wins. For full credit, name the orientation, give the scaling, and add one sentence on boundary layer growth.

Problem 4: Measuring a Fluid's Thermal Conductivity (18 pts)

A fluid flows through a pipe whose walls are at a constant, known temperature. Your task is to determine the fluid's thermal conductivity.

ParameterVariableValue
Tube length between A and B$L$2.5 m
Tube inner diameter$D$0.02 m
Fluid density$\rho$900 kg/m³
Fluid specific heat$c_p$2000 J/kg·K
Fluid viscosity$\mu$0.01 N·s/m²
Bulk temperature at the inlet$T_b(0)$15°C
Bulk temperature at the outlet$T_b(L)$45°C
Wall surface temperature$T_{wall}$100°C

Tests: the laminar limit, backing $\bar h$ out of the constant-$T_s$ exponential (sheet p. 7), and the fact that fully developed laminar $Nu$ is a constant (sheet p. 8). Note that $k_f$ is the unknown, so you can't compute $Pr$ or $Nu$ from properties.

(a) 3 pts. What is the maximum velocity for which the flow is still laminar?

Hint
Set $Re_D$ equal to the critical value on the sheet.
Show solution (a)
$$Re_D = \frac{\rho u_mD}{\mu} = 2300\ \Rightarrow\ u_{max} = \frac{2300\mu}{\rho D} = \frac{2300(0.01)}{900(0.02)}$$
$u_{max} \approx 1.28$ m/s

(b) 8 pts. To make sure the flow is laminar, you use 25% of the velocity from (a). At this velocity the outlet temperature is 45°C. Determine the average heat transfer coefficient $\bar h$. (If you didn't get (a), assume 1.2 m/s.)

Hint
Constant wall temperature means the exponential $T_m(x)$ relation. You know both ends, so the only unknown in it is $\bar h$. Take the log.
Show solution (b)

Flow

$$u_m = 0.25(1.278) = 0.319\ \text{m/s}\ \ (Re_D = 575)$$ $$\dot m = \rho u_m\frac{\pi D^2}{4} = 900(0.319)\frac{\pi(0.02)^2}{4} = 0.0903\ \text{kg/s}$$

Symbolic

$$\frac{T_s - T_b(L)}{T_s - T_b(0)} = \exp\left(-\frac{\pi DL\bar h}{\dot mc_p}\right)$$ $$\bar h = \frac{\dot mc_p}{\pi DL}\ln\frac{T_s - T_b(0)}{T_s - T_b(L)} = \frac{\rho u_mc_pD}{4L}\ln\frac{T_s - T_b(0)}{T_s - T_b(L)}$$

Numbers

$$\bar h = \frac{0.0903(2000)}{\pi(0.02)(2.5)}\ln\frac{100 - 15}{100 - 45} = 1150\ln(1.545) = 1150(0.4353)$$

Same answer by the LMTD route

$$q = \dot mc_p\Delta T_b = 0.0903(2000)(30) = 5419\ \text{W}$$ $$\Delta T_{lm} = \frac{85 - 55}{\ln(85/55)} = 68.9\ \text{K},\quad \bar h = \frac{q}{\pi DL\,\Delta T_{lm}} = 501$$
$\bar h \approx 501$ W/m²·K (with the 1.2 m/s fallback: $u_m = 0.30$ m/s, $\bar h \approx 470$ W/m²·K)
Using the arithmetic-mean $\Delta T$ (70 K) gives 493 W/m²·K. It's close here, but it's the wrong method and graders take points for it.

(c) 7 pts. Determine the fluid's thermal conductivity $k_f$. Assume the flow is fully developed, so $h$ is the same the whole way along the pipe and equals $\bar h$. (Hint: remember that it is laminar.)

Hint
Laminar + fully developed + constant wall temperature: what is $Nu_D$? It doesn't depend on $Re$ or $Pr$, which is exactly why this works without knowing $k_f$ first.
Show solution (c)

Symbolic

$$Nu_D = \frac{\bar hD}{k_f} = 3.66\ \Rightarrow\ k_f = \frac{\bar hD}{3.66}$$

Numbers

$$k_f = \frac{501(0.02)}{3.66}$$
$k_f \approx 2.74$ W/m·K (fallback: 2.57 W/m·K)
$Re_D = 575 \lt 2300$, so laminar is confirmed. The resulting $Pr = \mu c_p/k_f = 7.3$ is a plausible liquid value.

Going further (not needed for the exam): with this $k_f$, $x_{fd,t} = 0.05Re_DPr\,D \approx 4.2$ m, longer than the 2.5 m tube. So the flow isn't really thermally developed, and the true $h$ is above $3.66k/D$. Using the thermal-entry correlation from the sheet instead gives $k_f \approx 1.7$ W/m·K. The problem tells you to assume fully developed, so 2.74 is the expected answer, but a one-line remark like this shows you checked the assumption.

Takeaways for the Real Exam

  1. Regime check first, every time. This exam has five $Re$ checks, and each one changed the correlation: turbulent water vs. laminar oil, mixed vs. laminar plate, and the 2300 limit itself.
  2. Annulus bookkeeping: $D_i$ is the inner pipe's outside diameter. Use $Re = \dot mD_h/(A_c\mu)$ and $Nu = hD_h/k$. Each fluid's area in $1/UA$ is the surface it actually touches.
  3. Look at which resistance dominates. One weak side (laminar oil, gas) sets $UA$. A table of resistance shares takes one minute and is a strong "explain" answer.
  4. Balanced counterflow ($C_r = 1$): $\varepsilon = NTU/(1 + NTU)$ and $\Delta T$ is constant along the exchanger. The general formula is 0/0.
  5. How $\bar h$ scales with $L$: laminar $L^{-1/2}$, turbulent $L^{-1/5}$. Shorter is better unless the longer plate gets to trip into turbulence.
  6. Backing $\bar h$ out of data: constant-$T_s$ exponential or LMTD, never the arithmetic mean. Fully developed laminar $Nu$ is a constant, which is what lets you get $k_f$.
  7. "We assumed X" questions want a comparison: entry length vs. tube length, film temperature vs. property temperature, $Re$ vs. the critical value.
  8. Symbolic first. 1(d) gives full credit for the symbolic $L$ alone. Carry symbols through and plug in numbers once at the end.

Solving Checklist (any convection problem)

  1. Sketch it and write Known / Find. Label every temperature.
  2. Classify: external or internal? Forced or free? Which geometry? Plates, cylinders and spheres are Ch 7; tubes and ducts are Ch 8; buoyancy is Ch 9; two fluids are Ch 11.
  3. Property temperature: $T_f$ for external flow and free convection, $\bar T_m$ for internal flow, $T_\infty$ for Zukauskas and Whitaker. Guess if it's unknown.
  4. Compute the regime number: $Re$ (compare with $5\times10^5$ for a plate, 2300 for a tube) or $Ra$ (compare with $10^9$ for a vertical plate).
  5. Pick the correlation and check its validity limits. Get $\overline{Nu}$, then $\bar h = \overline{Nu}\,k/L_c$. Use the right characteristic length ($L$, $D$, $D_h$, $A_s/P$).
  6. Energy balance: $q = \bar hA\Delta T$, plus radiation if a surface is exposed. Use $q = \dot mc_p\Delta T_m$ with the exponential or LMTD for tubes. Use LMTD or ε–NTU for exchangers.
  7. Check: go back and test the assumed temperature, the regime, and whether the answer makes physical sense ($T_{c,o} \lt T_{h,i}$, $\varepsilon \le 1$, $h$ in the expected range).

Common Mistakes

  • Using °C in $\beta = 1/T$ or in radiation ($T^4$). Always use kelvin.
  • Using laminar correlations without checking $Re$ (or using the mixed one when the problem says "assume turbulent").
  • Forgetting that a plate in parallel flow loses heat from both sides.
  • Measuring $x$ from the start of heating instead of the plate's leading edge (unheated starting length).
  • Multiplying $\bar h$ by the whole plate area when only part of it is heated.
  • Evaluating tube properties at $T_f$ instead of $\bar T_m$, and not iterating.
  • Using the arithmetic mean $\Delta T$ in place of the LMTD.
  • Mixing up $\Delta T_1$ and $\Delta T_2$ for counterflow vs. parallel flow.
  • Using $C_{max}$ in $q_{max}$ or NTU.
  • Dittus–Boelter: $n = 0.4$ for heating and $0.3$ for cooling the fluid.
  • Annulus: $D_h = D_o - D_i$ in $Re$ and $Nu$, but the heat transfer area is still $\pi D_iL$.
  • Horizontal plates: $L = A_s/P$, not the side length.
  • Leaving radiation out when a surface sees large surroundings (it's often as big as free convection).
  • Getting $Re_D = 4\dot m/(\pi D\mu)$ wrong for an annulus. There you use $\dot mD_h/(A_c\mu)$.

Practice Midterm, Part A: Short Questions

Aim for about 3 minutes each.

A1 Laminar or mixed? Ch 7

Air ($\nu = 1.6\times10^{-5}$ m²/s) flows at 5 m/s over a 1.5 m plate. Which correlation do you use for $\bar h$?

Show answer

$Re_L = 5(1.5)/1.6\times10^{-5} = 4.69\times10^5 \lt 5\times10^5$

Laminar over the whole plate: $\overline{Nu}_L = 0.664Re_L^{1/2}Pr^{1/3}$

A2 Cooling rate of a plate Ch 7

A 5-mm steel plate ($\rho = 7850$ kg/m³, $c = 460$ J/kg·K) at 220°C is cooled on both faces by 20°C air with $\bar h = 20$ W/m²·K. Neglecting radiation, find the initial $dT/dt$.

Show answer

$\rho(A\delta)c\,\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -2\bar hA(T - T_\infty)$, so $\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -\dfrac{2(20)(200)}{7850(460)(0.005)}$.

$dT/dt \approx -0.44$ K/s

A3 Unheated starting length bookkeeping Ch 7

A 1 m plate is heated only over its last 0.6 m. When you compute $Re_x$ at the trailing edge, what is $x$? What area goes in $q = \bar hA\Delta T$?

Show answer
$x = 1$ m (from the plate's leading edge, where the velocity boundary layer starts); $A = 0.6W$ (heated part only)

A4 Bigger cylinder Ch 7

A cylinder in cross flow has $Re_D \approx 10^4$ (Hilpert $m = 0.618$). If $D$ doubles at the same velocity, by what factor do $\bar h$ and $q'$ change?

Show answer

$\bar h \propto D^{m-1}$ and $q' = \bar h\pi D\Delta T \propto D^m$.

$\bar h$: $2^{-0.382} = 0.77\times$; $q'$: $2^{0.618} = 1.53\times$

A5 Fully developed? Ch 8

Turbulent water flows through a 15 mm tube 3 m long. Can you use a fully developed correlation for the whole tube?

Show answer
Yes. $L/D = 200$, and turbulent entry lengths are about $10D$, so entry effects are small.

A6 Constant wall temperature Ch 8

$T_s = 100^\circ$C, $T_{m,i} = 20^\circ$C, $\bar hPL/(\dot mc_p) = 0.5$. Find $T_{m,o}$. What if the flow rate is halved (assume $\bar h$ unchanged)?

Show answer

$T_{m,o} = 100 - 80e^{-0.5} = 51.5^\circ$C. Halving $\dot m$ doubles the exponent: $100 - 80e^{-1} = 70.6^\circ$C.

51.5°C → 70.6°C. In reality $\bar h$ also drops when the flow is halved (turbulent), so the outlet ends up a bit lower.

A7 Linearly increasing flux Ch 8

For $q_s'' = ax$ with constant $h$, what shapes do $T_m(x)$ and $T_s(x) - T_m(x)$ have?

Show answer

$\dot mc_p\,dT_m/dx = a x P$, so $T_m = T_{m,i} + \dfrac{aP}{2\dot mc_p}x^2$ (a parabola). $T_s - T_m = ax/h$ is linear and grows along the tube, so the hottest wall is at the outlet.

A8 Oil entry length Ch 8

Oil ($Pr = 1000$) flows at $Re_D = 100$ in a 20 mm tube 10 m long with constant $T_s$. Which correlation do you use?

Show answer

$x_{fd,t} = 0.05(100)(1000)(0.02) = 100$ m, which is far longer than the tube.

The whole tube is in the thermal entry region. Use Hausen (8.57), not $Nu = 3.66$.

A9 Horizontal plate length scale Ch 9

A 0.5 m × 0.5 m horizontal hot plate faces up. What characteristic length goes in $Ra_L$?

Show answer
$L = A_s/P = 0.25/2 = 0.125$ m

A10 Forced, free, or mixed? Ch 9

A vertical plate 0.5 m tall is 30 K above the air temperature ($T_f \approx 300$ K, $\nu = 15.89\times10^{-6}$ m²/s). A gentle upward draft of 0.3 m/s flows along it. Which regime is it?

Show answer

$Gr_L = \dfrac{9.8(1/300)(30)(0.5)^3}{(15.89\times10^{-6})^2} = 4.9\times10^8$ and $Re_L = 0.3(0.5)/15.89\times10^{-6} = 9440$, so $Gr_L/Re_L^2 = 5.4$.

$Gr/Re^2 \approx 5$: buoyancy dominates, but this is close to mixed. Free convection can't be ignored.

A11 Quick effectiveness Ch 11

A counterflow exchanger has $C_{min} = 500$ W/K, $C_{max} = 1000$ W/K and $UA = 500$ W/K. Inlets are 100°C and 20°C. Find $\varepsilon$ and $q$.

Show answer

$NTU = 1$ and $C_r = 0.5$, so $\varepsilon = \dfrac{1 - e^{-0.5}}{1 - 0.5e^{-0.5}} = 0.565$.

$\varepsilon = 0.565$; $q = 0.565(500)(80) = 22.6$ kW

A12 Same temperatures, two arrangements Ch 11

Hot fluid 80 → 50°C, cold fluid 20 → 40°C. Find $\Delta T_{lm}$ for counterflow and for parallel flow. Which needs less area?

Show answer

Counterflow: $\Delta T_1 = 80 - 40 = 40$ and $\Delta T_2 = 50 - 20 = 30$, so $\Delta T_{lm} = 34.8$ K.

Parallel: $\Delta T_1 = 80 - 20 = 60$ and $\Delta T_2 = 50 - 40 = 10$, so $\Delta T_{lm} = 27.9$ K.

Counterflow (larger $\Delta T_{lm}$ means less area for the same $q$ and $U$): about 20% less

Practice Midterm, Part B: Long Problems

Aim for 20–25 minutes each. Look up air in Table A.4, water in A.6 and oil in A.5. The values used are in each solution.

M1 Hot steel sheet cooled by air and radiation Ch 7 like 7.24, 7.20

A steel sheet 0.8 m × 0.8 m × 4 mm ($\rho = 7830$ kg/m³, $c = 560$ J/kg·K, $\varepsilon = 0.7$) leaves a furnace at 400°C. Air at 25°C blows over both faces in parallel flow at 8 m/s. The surroundings are at 25°C.

  1. Find the convection coefficient and the initial heat loss by convection and by radiation.
  2. Find the initial cooling rate $dT/dt$.
  3. Which mode dominates? Will that change as the sheet cools?
Show solution

Properties

$T_f = (673 + 298)/2 = 485.5$ K: $\nu = 36.93\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.0397$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.685$.

(a) Coefficient and heat loss

$Re_L = 8(0.8)/36.93\times10^{-6} = 1.73\times10^5$, so laminar.

$$\overline{Nu}_L = 0.664(1.73\times10^5)^{1/2}(0.685)^{1/3} = 244 \Rightarrow \bar h = \frac{244(0.0397)}{0.8} = 12.1\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}$$

Both faces: $A = 2(0.64) = 1.28$ m².

Convection: $q_{conv} = 12.1(1.28)(375) = 5.80$ kW

Radiation: $q_{rad} = 0.7(5.67\times10^{-8})(1.28)(673^4 - 298^4) = 10.0$ kW

(b) Cooling rate

Mass: $m = 7830(0.64)(0.004) = 20.0$ kg

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = -\frac{q_{conv} + q_{rad}}{mc} = -\frac{15{,}830}{20.0(560)} = -1.41\ \text{K/s}$$
$\bar h = 12.1$ W/m²·K; $q_{conv} = 5.8$ kW, $q_{rad} = 10.0$ kW; $dT/dt \approx -1.4$ K/s; radiation dominates at first, but since it scales with $T^4$ it falls off faster, and convection takes over at lower temperatures

Comment: lumped capacitance holds because $Bi = \bar h(\delta/2)/k_{steel} \approx 10^{-3}$.

M2 Heating wire: find the surface temperature Ch 7 cylinder ⚑

An electric wire of diameter 3 mm dissipates 100 W per meter. Air at 20°C flows across it at 10 m/s. Find the wire's surface temperature. (Properties depend on the answer, so iterate.)

Show solution

Pass 1: guess $T_s = 60^\circ$C, so $T_f = 313$ K

$\nu = 17.20\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.0273$, $Pr = 0.705$

$Re_D = 10(0.003)/17.20\times10^{-6} = 1744$. Churchill–Bernstein gives $\overline{Nu}_D = 21.2$, so $\bar h = 193$ W/m²·K.

$T_s = T_\infty + \dfrac{q'}{\bar h\pi D} = 20 + \dfrac{100}{193\pi(0.003)} = 75.0^\circ$C

Pass 2: $T_f = (75 + 20)/2 = 47.5^\circ$C $\approx 320.5$ K

$\nu = 17.95\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.0278$, $Pr = 0.704$

$Re_D = 1671$, $\overline{Nu}_D = 20.7$, $\bar h = 192$ W/m²·K, so $T_s = 75.2^\circ$C.

$T_s \approx 75^\circ$C (converged after one update)

Comment: $\bar h$ barely changes with property temperature for gases, so one iteration is usually enough. Say so on the exam.

M3 Engine oil heated in a tube (laminar entry) Ch 8 like 8.25

Engine oil at 0.1 kg/s enters a 25-mm-diameter, 10-m-long tube at 30°C. The tube wall is held at 110°C.

  1. Is the flow laminar? Is it thermally fully developed?
  2. Find the outlet temperature and heat rate.
  3. If the flow rate were raised to 0.4 kg/s, would $q$ go up or down? Would $T_{m,o}$?
Show solution

Pass 1: guess $T_{m,o} \approx 60^\circ$C, so $\bar T_m \approx 320$ K

$c_p = 1993$, $\mu = 0.141$, $k = 0.143$, $Pr = 1965$. This gives $Re_D = 36$, $\overline{Nu}_D = 8.9$ and $T_{m,o} = 44.5^\circ$C, so the guess was too high.

Pass 2: $\bar T_m \approx 37^\circ$C $\approx 310$ K

$c_p = 1951$ J/kg·K, $\mu = 0.253$ N·s/m², $k = 0.145$ W/m·K, $Pr = 3400$

(a) Regime and entry length

$Re_D = \dfrac{4(0.1)}{\pi(0.025)(0.253)} = 20.1$, very laminar.

$x_{fd,h} = 0.05Re_DD = 0.025$ m, so the velocity profile develops almost immediately. But $x_{fd,t} = 0.05Re_DPr\,D = 86$ m $\gg 10$ m: the tube is entirely in the thermal entry region. That calls for Hausen.

(b) Outlet temperature and heat rate

$$Gz_D = \frac{D}{L}Re_DPr = \frac{0.025}{10}(20.1)(3400) = 171$$ $$\overline{Nu}_D = 3.66 + \frac{0.0668(171)}{1 + 0.04(171)^{2/3}} = 8.78 \Rightarrow \bar h = \frac{8.78(0.145)}{0.025} = 50.9\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}$$ $$T_{m,o} = 110 - 80\exp\left[-\frac{50.9\pi(0.025)(10)}{0.1(1951)}\right] = 110 - 80e^{-0.205} = 44.8^\circ\text{C}$$

$q = 0.1(1951)(44.8 - 30) = 2.89$ kW

(c) Four times the flow rate

$Gz$ rises to 684, giving $\overline{Nu} = 14.8$, $\bar h = 86$ W/m²·K and $T_{m,o} = 36.6^\circ$C. Then $q = 0.4(1951)(6.6) = 5.2$ kW.

(a) laminar ($Re \approx 20$), with the whole tube in the thermal entry region; (b) $T_{m,o} \approx 45^\circ$C, $q \approx 2.9$ kW; (c) $q$ rises (to about 5.2 kW) but $T_{m,o}$ falls (to about 37°C)

Comment: this trade-off is exactly what HW 8.25(b) asks you to explain. More flow means a higher $h$ and a larger average $\Delta T$, but less time in the tube for each kilogram of oil.

M4 Measuring $k$ and $\varepsilon$ Ch 9 like 9.39

A sample 20 mm thick with a 0.2 m × 0.2 m square face sits on a heater. The sides and bottom are insulated. The top face is exposed to quiescent air and large surroundings, both at 20°C. At steady state with 32 W of heater power, the bottom of the sample is at 120°C and the top at 80°C. Find the sample's thermal conductivity and its top-surface emissivity.

Show solution

Conduction through the sample

All of the heater power conducts through the sample:

$$k = \frac{P\,t}{A(T_1 - T_2)} = \frac{32(0.02)}{0.04(40)} = 0.40\ \text{W/m}\cdot\text{K}$$

Free convection from the top (hot surface facing up)

$L = A_s/P = 0.04/0.8 = 0.05$ m. Properties at $T_f = 50^\circ$C (323 K): $\nu = 18.20\times10^{-6}$, $\alpha = 25.9\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.0280$, $Pr = 0.704$.

$$Ra_L = \frac{9.8(1/323)(60)(0.05)^3}{(18.20\times10^{-6})(25.9\times10^{-6})} = 4.83\times10^5$$

$\overline{Nu}_L = 0.54Ra_L^{1/4} = 14.2$, so $\bar h = 14.2(0.028)/0.05 = 7.97$ W/m²·K.

$q_{conv} = 7.97(0.04)(60) = 19.1$ W

Radiation gets the rest

$q_{rad} = 32 - 19.1 = 12.9$ W

$$\varepsilon = \frac{q_{rad}}{\sigma A(T_2^4 - T_{sur}^4)} = \frac{12.9}{5.67\times10^{-8}(0.04)(353^4 - 293^4)} = \frac{12.9}{18.5}$$
$k = 0.40$ W/m·K; $\varepsilon \approx 0.70$

M5 Concentric tube exchanger from scratch Ch 8 + 11 like 11.17, 11.57

A concentric tube exchanger is 6 m long. The inner tube is thin-walled with $D_i = 20$ mm; the outer tube has $D_o = 40$ mm and is insulated outside. Cold water (0.1 kg/s, 15°C) flows in the inner tube. Hot water (0.15 kg/s, 80°C) flows in the annulus.

  1. Find $h$ for each stream and the overall $U$. Assume fully developed flow; evaluate properties at about 30°C (cold) and 70°C (hot).
  2. Find the outlet temperatures and $q$ for counterflow.
  3. Repeat (b) for parallel flow.
Show solution

Properties

Cold water (303 K): $c_p = 4178$, $\mu = 803\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.617$, $Pr = 5.45$

Hot water (343 K): $c_p = 4190$, $\mu = 401\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.662$, $Pr = 2.53$

(a) Inner tube

$Re_D = \dfrac{4(0.1)}{\pi(0.02)(803\times10^{-6})} = 7930$, turbulent but below $10^4$, so use Gnielinski: $f = (0.790\ln7930 - 1.64)^{-2} = 0.0336$, giving $Nu_D = 58.2$ and $h_c = 58.2(0.617)/0.02 = 1800$ W/m²·K.

(a) Annulus

$D_h = D_o - D_i = 0.02$ m and $A_c = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.04^2 - 0.02^2) = 9.42\times10^{-4}$ m².

$Re_{D_h} = \dfrac{\dot mD_h}{A_c\mu} = \dfrac{0.15(0.02)}{9.42\times10^{-4}(401\times10^{-6})} = 7940$. Gnielinski with $D_h$ (an approximation for turbulent annuli) gives $Nu = 43.3$, so $h_h = 43.3(0.662)/0.02 = 1430$ W/m²·K.

(a) Overall coefficient

$U = (1/1800 + 1/1430)^{-1} = 797$ W/m²·K

$A = \pi D_iL = 0.377$ m², so $UA = 300$ W/K

(b) Counterflow

$C_c = 418$ W/K ($= C_{min}$) and $C_h = 629$ W/K, so $C_r = 0.665$ and $NTU = 300/418 = 0.719$.

$\varepsilon_{CF} = 0.448$, so $q = 0.448(418)(65) = 12.2$ kW.

$T_{c,o} = 15 + 12200/418 = 44.2^\circ$C and $T_{h,o} = 80 - 12200/629 = 60.6^\circ$C.

(c) Parallel flow

$\varepsilon_{PF} = 0.419$, so $q = 11.4$ kW, $T_{c,o} = 42.2^\circ$C and $T_{h,o} = 61.9^\circ$C.

Check property temperatures: $\bar T_c = (15 + 44)/2 \approx 30^\circ$C ✓ and $\bar T_h = (80 + 61)/2 \approx 70^\circ$C ✓

$h_c \approx 1800$, $h_h \approx 1430$, $U \approx 800$ W/m²·K; counterflow: $q = 12.2$ kW, cold out at 44.2°C, hot out at 60.6°C; parallel: $q = 11.4$ kW, cold out at 42.2°C, hot out at 61.9°C

Comment: at $NTU \approx 0.7$ the two arrangements are close (7% apart). The counterflow advantage grows with NTU.

M6 Shell-and-tube: back out the shell-side coefficient Ch 11 like 11.58

A shell-and-tube exchanger has one shell pass and 8 tube passes of a single copper tube ($D_i = 20$ mm, $D_o = 24$ mm, 3 m per pass, $k = 400$ W/m·K). Hot water at 0.25 kg/s is cooled from 90°C to 50°C in the tube. Oil ($\dot m = 0.8$ kg/s, $c_p = 2100$ J/kg·K) on the shell side enters at 15°C. Find the oil outlet temperature, $UA$, and the shell-side convection coefficient $h_o$.

Show solution

Energy balance

$C_h = 0.25(4195) = 1049$ W/K ($= C_{min}$) and $C_c = 1680$ W/K.

$q = 1049(40) = 41.95$ kW, so $T_{c,o} = 15 + 41950/1680 = 40.0^\circ$C.

ε–NTU for one shell pass

$C_r = 0.624$ and $\varepsilon = 41950/[1049(75)] = 0.533$.

$$E = \frac{2/\varepsilon - (1 + C_r)}{(1 + C_r^2)^{1/2}} = 1.803,\qquad NTU = -(1 + C_r^2)^{-1/2}\ln\frac{E - 1}{E + 1} = 1.060$$

$UA = 1.060(1049) = 1112$ W/K

Tube-side coefficient

Water at about 70°C: $\mu = 401\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.662$, $Pr = 2.53$.

$Re_D = \dfrac{4(0.25)}{\pi(0.02)(401\times10^{-6})} = 39{,}700$. Dittus–Boelter with $n = 0.3$ (water is cooling) gives $Nu = 145$, so $h_i = 4800$ W/m²·K.

Resistance network

Total tube length is 24 m, so $A_i = \pi(0.02)(24) = 1.508$ m² and $A_o = \pi(0.024)(24) = 1.810$ m².

$$\frac{1}{UA} = \frac{1}{h_iA_i} + \frac{\ln(D_o/D_i)}{2\pi kL} + \frac{1}{h_oA_o}$$ $$8.99\times10^{-4} = 1.38\times10^{-4} + 3.0\times10^{-6} + \frac{1}{h_o(1.810)}$$
$T_{c,o} = 40^\circ$C; $UA \approx 1110$ W/K; $h_o \approx 730$ W/m²·K

Check with LMTD: $\Delta T_{lm,CF} = 42.1$ K, so $F = q/(UA\,\Delta T_{lm,CF}) = 0.90$. That's consistent with Fig. 11.10 for $P = 0.33$, $R = 1.6$. The wall resistance is negligible, and the shell side controls.

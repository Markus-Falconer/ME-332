Ch 9: Free Convection

Buoyancy-driven flow: Grashof and Rayleigh numbers, plates, cylinders, fin spacing, and when free convection matters.

Jump to: ConceptsEquationsShort questionsLong problems

Key Concepts

  • Free (natural) convection is driven by density differences from temperature differences in a gravity field. The fluid velocity is zero at the wall and zero far away, with a peak inside the boundary layer. Velocities and $h$ are small: typically $h \approx 2$–$10$ W/m²·K in air.
  • Boussinesq approximation: density only varies in the buoyancy term, through $\beta(T - T_\infty)$. For an ideal gas $\beta = 1/T$, with $T$ in kelvin (use $T_f$).
  • Grashof number $Gr_L = g\beta(T_s - T_\infty)L^3/\nu^2$ is the ratio of buoyancy to viscous forces. It plays the role $Re^2$ plays in forced convection. The Rayleigh number is $Ra_L = Gr_L Pr$.
  • Transition on a vertical plate happens at $Ra_{x,c} \approx 10^9$. In the turbulent regime $\overline{Nu} \propto Ra^{1/3} \propto L$, so $\bar h$ doesn't depend on $L$.
  • Laminar vertical plate (similarity solution): $\delta \propto x^{1/4}$, $h_x \propto x^{-1/4}$, and $\overline{Nu}_L = \frac{4}{3}Nu_L$. The boundary layer edge is near $\eta \approx 6$, so $\delta \approx 6x(Gr_x/4)^{-1/4}$.
  • Cold plates work the same way flipped: the boundary layer starts at the top and the flow goes down.
  • Horizontal plates: heat transfer is much better for the upper surface of a hot plate or the lower surface of a cold plate, because fluid can rise or sink away freely. It's weaker for the lower surface of a hot plate and the upper surface of a cold plate. Use $L = A_s/P$.
  • Vertical channels / fin arrays: narrower spacing means more fins but less flow between them, so there is an optimum spacing. A quick estimate is $S_{opt} \approx 2\delta$ at the top of the fin, where the boundary layers just meet (HW 9.9 method using Fig. 9.4).
  • Mixed convection: compare $Gr_L/Re_L^2$. If it's $\ll 1$, forced convection dominates. If $\gg 1$, free convection dominates. If $\approx 1$, consider both.
  • Radiation is often as large as free convection in air at moderate temperatures ($h_r \approx 5$–$7$ W/m²·K near room temperature with $\varepsilon \approx 0.9$). Most HW problems (9.19, 9.39) include it.
  • All properties are evaluated at the film temperature $T_f = (T_s + T_\infty)/2$.

Key Equations

Dimensionless groups ($\beta = 1/T_f$ for an ideal gas)
$$Gr_L = \frac{g\beta(T_s - T_\infty)L^3}{\nu^2},\qquad Ra_L = Gr_L Pr = \frac{g\beta(T_s - T_\infty)L^3}{\nu\alpha}$$
Similarity solution, laminar vertical plate
$$Nu_x = \left(\frac{Gr_x}{4}\right)^{1/4}g(Pr),\quad \overline{Nu}_L = \frac{4}{3}\left(\frac{Gr_L}{4}\right)^{1/4}g(Pr),\quad g(Pr) = \frac{0.75Pr^{1/2}}{(0.609 + 1.221Pr^{1/2} + 1.238Pr)^{1/4}}$$ $$\delta \approx 6x\left(\frac{Gr_x}{4}\right)^{-1/4}$$
Vertical plate: Churchill–Chu (all $Ra_L$), and the more accurate laminar form ($Ra_L \lesssim 10^9$)
$$\overline{Nu}_L = \left\{0.825 + \frac{0.387Ra_L^{1/6}}{[1 + (0.492/Pr)^{9/16}]^{8/27}}\right\}^2 \qquad \overline{Nu}_L = 0.68 + \frac{0.670Ra_L^{1/4}}{[1 + (0.492/Pr)^{9/16}]^{4/9}}$$
Horizontal plates ($L = A_s/P$)

Upper surface of a hot plate, or lower surface of a cold plate:

$$\overline{Nu}_L = 0.54Ra_L^{1/4}\ (10^4 \lesssim Ra_L \lesssim 10^7),\qquad \overline{Nu}_L = 0.15Ra_L^{1/3}\ (10^7 \lesssim Ra_L \lesssim 10^{11})$$

Lower surface of a hot plate, or upper surface of a cold plate:

$$\overline{Nu}_L = 0.52Ra_L^{1/5}\ (10^4 \lesssim Ra_L \lesssim 10^9)$$
Long horizontal cylinder (Churchill–Chu, $Ra_D \lesssim 10^{12}$) and sphere ($Ra_D \lesssim 10^{11}$, $Pr \gtrsim 0.7$)
$$\overline{Nu}_D = \left\{0.60 + \frac{0.387Ra_D^{1/6}}{[1 + (0.559/Pr)^{9/16}]^{8/27}}\right\}^2 \qquad \overline{Nu}_D = 2 + \frac{0.589Ra_D^{1/4}}{[1 + (0.469/Pr)^{9/16}]^{4/9}}$$
Vertical parallel plates, symmetric isothermal (Table 9.3)
$$S_{opt} = 2.71\left(\frac{Ra_S}{S^3L}\right)^{-1/4}\qquad \text{quick estimate: } S_{opt} \approx 2\delta(L)$$
Mixed convection check
$$\frac{Gr_L}{Re_L^2} \ll 1:\ \text{forced}\qquad \frac{Gr_L}{Re_L^2} \gg 1:\ \text{free}\qquad \frac{Gr_L}{Re_L^2} \approx 1:\ \text{mixed}$$

Short Questions

S1 Expansion coefficient

A plate at 60°C is in 20°C air. What value of $\beta$ do you use? What's the common mistake?

Show answer

$T_f = 40^\circ$C $= 313$ K, so $\beta = 1/313 = 3.19\times10^{-3}$ K⁻¹.

$\beta = 3.19\times10^{-3}$ K⁻¹. The mistake is using $1/40$ (°C), which is about 8× too large.

S2 Laminar or turbulent?

A 1-m-tall vertical wall is 40 K warmer than the surrounding air ($T_f = 300$ K: $\nu = 15.89\times10^{-6}$, $\alpha = 22.5\times10^{-6}$ m²/s). Laminar or turbulent?

Show answer
$$Ra_L = \frac{9.8(1/300)(40)(1)^3}{(15.89\times10^{-6})(22.5\times10^{-6})} = 3.7\times10^9$$
$Ra_L \gt 10^9$: turbulent over the upper part. Use the full Churchill–Chu correlation.

S3 Turbulent free convection and length

Why is $\bar h$ for turbulent free convection on a vertical plate independent of the plate height?

Show answer

$\overline{Nu}_L \propto Ra_L^{1/3} \propto (L^3)^{1/3} = L$, so $\bar h = \overline{Nu}_Lk/L$ has no $L$ left in it.

S4 Hot vs. cold horizontal plates

(a) For a hot horizontal plate, which surface has the larger $h$? (b) For a cold plate (say a chilled shelf), which surface behaves like the "good" case?

Show answer

(a) The top: warm fluid rises straight off it. On the bottom, warm fluid is trapped and has to flow out to the edges.

(b) The bottom of the cold plate: cool fluid sinks straight off it. Use $0.54Ra^{1/4}$ or $0.15Ra^{1/3}$ there, and $0.52Ra^{1/5}$ on top.

S5 Characteristic length

Find $L$ for free convection from (a) a 0.6 m × 0.3 m horizontal plate, (b) a horizontal disk of diameter $D$.

Show answer

(a) $L = A_s/P = 0.18/1.8 = 0.1$ m. (b) $L = (\pi D^2/4)/(\pi D) = D/4$.

S6 Temperature scaling

For laminar free convection from a vertical plate, how does the heat rate scale with $\Delta T$? What happens if $\Delta T$ doubles?

Show answer

$\bar h \propto Ra^{1/4} \propto \Delta T^{1/4}$, so $q = \bar hA\Delta T \propto \Delta T^{5/4}$.

$q \propto \Delta T^{5/4}$; doubling $\Delta T$ multiplies $q$ by $2^{1.25} = 2.38$

S7 Mixed convection

For a heated plate in a slow air stream, $Gr_L/Re_L^2 = 0.01$ in case A and 50 in case B. Which correlations do you use?

Show answer
A: forced convection (Ch. 7); buoyancy is negligible. B: free convection (Ch. 9); the imposed flow is negligible.

S8 Window on a winter night

On the room side of a cold window, which way does the boundary layer flow, and where does it start?

Show answer

Air next to the glass is cooled, gets denser, and sinks. The boundary layer starts at the top and flows down, which is the cold draft you feel near windows. Treat it as a vertical plate with $T_s \lt T_\infty$ (same correlation).

S9 Why an optimum fin spacing?

Explain briefly why an array of vertical fins in still air has an optimum spacing $S$.

Show answer

Smaller $S$ means more fins and more area. But if $S$ is less than about $2\delta$, neighboring boundary layers merge, flow between the fins is choked by viscous drag, and $\bar h$ falls. The product $\bar hA$ peaks at an intermediate $S$.

S10 Local vs. average

For the laminar similarity solution, how does $\overline{Nu}_L$ compare with the local $Nu_L$ at the trailing edge?

Show answer

$h_x \propto x^{-1/4}$, so $\bar h_L = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L h_x\,dx = \frac{4}{3}h_L$.

$\overline{Nu}_L = \frac{4}{3}Nu_L$

S11 Don't forget radiation

A painted surface ($\varepsilon = 0.9$) at 50°C sits in a 20°C room. Estimate $h_r$ and compare with a typical free convection $h$.

Show answer

$h_r = \varepsilon\sigma(T_s + T_{sur})(T_s^2 + T_{sur}^2) = 0.9(5.67\times10^{-8})(616)(323^2 + 293^2) = 6.0$ W/m²·K

$h_r \approx 6$ W/m²·K, about the same as free convection ($h \approx 3$–$6$). Radiation can't be neglected.

S12 Vertical cylinder

When can a vertical cylinder be treated with the vertical plate correlation?

Show answer

When the boundary layer is thin compared with the diameter: $D/L \gtrsim 35/Gr_L^{1/4}$.

Long Problems

L1 Vertical plate: similarity vs. correlations, plus radiation like 9.13, 9.19

A vertical plate 0.5 m tall and 0.4 m wide is held at 60°C in quiescent air at 20°C. Both faces are exposed. The surroundings are also at 20°C, and the plate has $\varepsilon = 0.85$.

  1. Find $Ra_L$. Is the flow laminar?
  2. Find $\bar h$ using (i) the similarity solution, (ii) the laminar correlation (9.27), (iii) Churchill–Chu (9.26).
  3. Find the total heat loss by convection and radiation.
  4. How thick is the boundary layer at the top of the plate?
Show solution

Properties

$T_f = 313$ K: $\nu = 17.20\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $\alpha = 24.4\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.0273$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.705$, $\beta = 3.195\times10^{-3}$ K⁻¹.

(a) Rayleigh number

$$Gr_L = \frac{9.8(3.195\times10^{-3})(40)(0.5)^3}{(17.20\times10^{-6})^2} = 5.29\times10^8,\qquad Ra_L = Gr_LPr = 3.73\times10^8$$

$Ra_L \lt 10^9$, so the flow is laminar over the whole plate.

(b) Three ways to get $\bar h$

(i) $g(0.705) = 0.5005$, so $\overline{Nu}_L = \frac{4}{3}(5.29\times10^8/4)^{1/4}(0.5005) = 71.6$ and $\bar h = 3.91$ W/m²·K.

(ii) $\overline{Nu}_L = 0.68 + \dfrac{0.670(3.73\times10^8)^{1/4}}{[1+(0.492/0.705)^{9/16}]^{4/9}} = 72.1$, so $\bar h = 3.94$ W/m²·K.

(iii) $\overline{Nu}_L = \{0.825 + 0.387Ra_L^{1/6}/[\ldots]^{8/27}\}^2 = 90.7$, so $\bar h = 4.95$ W/m²·K.

(c) Total heat loss (both faces, $A = 2(0.5)(0.4) = 0.4$ m²)

Convection: $q_{conv} = 3.94(0.4)(40) = 63$ W

Radiation: $q_{rad} = 0.85(5.67\times10^{-8})(0.4)(333^4 - 293^4) = 95$ W

(d) Boundary layer thickness

$\delta_L \approx 6L(Gr_L/4)^{-1/4} = 6(0.5)/(1.32\times10^8)^{1/4} = 0.028$ m

$Ra_L = 3.7\times10^8$ (laminar); $\bar h \approx 3.9$ (similarity), 3.9 (eq. 9.27), 5.0 W/m²·K (eq. 9.26); $q \approx 63 + 95 = 158$ W; $\delta \approx 28$ mm

Comment: eq. 9.27 matches the similarity solution because both are laminar results. Eq. 9.26 runs high in the laminar range. Radiation is larger than convection here. For a window like HW 9.19, write this balance for each side and solve for the glass temperature by iteration.

L2 Horizontal plate: top vs. bottom like 9.39

A thin, square horizontal plate 0.4 m on a side is held at 80°C in still air at 20°C. Both faces are exposed.

  1. Find $\bar h$ for the top and bottom surfaces.
  2. Find the total convection heat loss.
  3. The plate is instead chilled to 0°C. Which correlation applies to each face now? Find both $h$ values.
Show solution

Setup

$L = A_s/P = 0.16/1.6 = 0.1$ m.

Properties at $T_f = 323$ K: $\nu = 18.20\times10^{-6}$, $\alpha = 25.9\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.0280$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.704$, $\beta = 1/323$ K⁻¹.

$$Ra_L = \frac{9.8(1/323)(60)(0.1)^3}{(18.20\times10^{-6})(25.9\times10^{-6})} = 3.86\times10^6$$

(a) Top and bottom

Top (hot surface up, $10^4 \lt Ra \lt 10^7$): $\overline{Nu}_L = 0.54Ra_L^{1/4} = 23.9$, so $\bar h_{top} = 23.9(0.028)/0.1 = 6.70$ W/m²·K.

Bottom (hot surface down): $\overline{Nu}_L = 0.52Ra_L^{1/5} = 10.8$, so $\bar h_{bot} = 3.02$ W/m²·K.

(b) Total convection

$q = (\bar h_{top} + \bar h_{bot})A\Delta T = (6.70 + 3.02)(0.16)(60) = 93$ W. The top carries about 69% of it.

(c) Chilled plate

Now the bottom is the good surface (cold air sinks away) and the top is the poor one. At $T_f = 283$ K, $Ra_L = 2.37\times10^6$. Bottom: $0.54Ra_L^{1/4}$ gives $\bar h = 5.3$ W/m²·K. Top: $0.52Ra_L^{1/5}$ gives $\bar h = 2.4$ W/m²·K.

(a) $\bar h_{top} = 6.7$, $\bar h_{bot} = 3.0$ W/m²·K; (b) $q \approx 93$ W; (c) the roles swap: bottom ≈ 5.3, top ≈ 2.4 W/m²·K

L3 Hot pipe in a room, then a fan

A horizontal, uninsulated hot-water pipe (OD 60 mm, $\varepsilon = 0.9$) has a surface temperature of 70°C in a room whose air and walls are at 20°C.

  1. Find the heat loss per meter by free convection and by radiation.
  2. A fan now blows room air across the pipe at 2 m/s. Is free convection still important? Find the new convection loss.
Show solution

Properties

$T_f = 318$ K: $\nu = 17.70\times10^{-6}$, $\alpha = 25.2\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.0276$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.704$.

(a) Still air

$$Ra_D = \frac{9.8(1/318)(50)(0.06)^3}{(17.70\times10^{-6})(25.2\times10^{-6})} = 7.46\times10^5$$ $$\overline{Nu}_D = \left\{0.60 + \frac{0.387(7.46\times10^5)^{1/6}}{[1 + (0.559/0.704)^{9/16}]^{8/27}}\right\}^2 = 13.4 \Rightarrow \bar h = 6.15\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}$$

Convection: $q'_{conv} = 6.15\pi(0.06)(50) = 58$ W/m

Radiation: $q'_{rad} = 0.9(5.67\times10^{-8})\pi(0.06)(343^4 - 293^4) = 62$ W/m

(b) With the fan

$Re_D = 2(0.06)/17.70\times10^{-6} = 6780$ and $Gr_D = Ra_D/Pr = 1.06\times10^6$, so $Gr_D/Re_D^2 = 0.023 \ll 1$. Forced convection dominates.

Churchill–Bernstein: $\overline{Nu}_D = 43.2$, giving $\bar h = 19.9$ W/m²·K and $q'_{conv} = 187$ W/m.

(a) $q' \approx 58 + 62 = 120$ W/m; (b) $Gr/Re^2 = 0.02$, so forced convection dominates and the convection loss rises to about 187 W/m (3.2×)

L4 Optimum spacing for a vertical fin array like 9.9

A heat sink has vertical rectangular fins, each $H = 100$ mm tall (the flow direction) and protruding $L = 30$ mm from the base. The fins are $t = 1$ mm thick and approximately isothermal at 70°C. The air is quiescent at 20°C, and the base is $W = 300$ mm wide.

  1. Estimate the optimum spacing from the boundary layer thickness ($S_{opt} \approx 2\delta$ at the top of the fin).
  2. How many fins fit, and what's the heat rate from the fins? Treat each fin face as an isolated vertical plate.
Show solution

Properties

$T_f = 318$ K: $\nu = 17.70\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.0276$, $Pr = 0.704$, $\beta = 1/318$.

(a) Spacing

$$Gr_H = \frac{9.8(1/318)(50)(0.1)^3}{(17.70\times10^{-6})^2} = 4.92\times10^6$$ $$\delta(H) \approx 6H\left(\frac{Gr_H}{4}\right)^{-1/4} = \frac{6(0.1)}{(1.23\times10^6)^{1/4}} = 18\ \text{mm} \Rightarrow S_{opt} \approx 36\ \text{mm}$$

(b) Fin count and heat rate

$N = W/(S + t) = 300/37 = 8.1$, so 8 fins.

$Ra_H = 3.46\times10^6$. Eq. 9.27 gives $\overline{Nu}_H = 22.8$, so $\bar h = 22.8(0.0276)/0.1 = 6.30$ W/m²·K.

$$q = N(2HL)\bar h(T_s - T_\infty) = 8(2)(0.1)(0.03)(6.30)(50) = 15.1\ \text{W}$$
$S_{opt} \approx 36$ mm; 8 fins; $q \approx 15$ W from the fins (base area not included)

Going further: the channel correlation in Table 9.3, $S_{opt} = 2.71(Ra_S/S^3L)^{-1/4}$, gives a smaller value (about 6 mm here). That's because it accounts for chimney flow between the fins. Use whichever method your professor asks for. HW 9.9 specifies the Fig. 9.4 (boundary layer thickness) approach.

Ch 7: External Flow

Forced convection over flat plates, cylinders and spheres: how to choose and use the correlations.

Jump to: ConceptsEquationsShort questionsLong problems

Key Concepts

  • External flow means the boundary layer grows freely, and outside it there's a free stream at $u_\infty, T_\infty$ with no gradients.
  • Every convection problem follows the same recipe: pick the geometry → evaluate properties at the right temperature → compute $Re$ (and $Pr$) → pick the correlation for the flow regime → get $\overline{Nu}$ → $\bar h = \overline{Nu}\,k/L$ → $q = \bar h A (T_s - T_\infty)$.
  • Film temperature: most external-flow correlations use properties at $T_f = (T_s + T_\infty)/2$. The exceptions are Zukauskas (cylinder) and Whitaker (sphere), which use $T_\infty$ plus a surface correction ($Pr_s$ or $\mu_s$).
  • Flat plate transition happens at $Re_{x,c} = 5\times10^5$. If $Re_L$ is below this, the plate is laminar everywhere. If it's above, you have a mixed boundary layer: laminar up to $x_c$, then turbulent.
  • How things scale on a flat plate: laminar $\delta \propto x^{1/2}$, $h_x \propto x^{-1/2}$ (so $\bar h_L = 2h_L$); turbulent $\delta \propto x^{4/5}$, $h_x \propto x^{-1/5}$ (so $\bar h_L = 1.25 h_L$). $h$ jumps up sharply at transition.
  • Relative boundary layer thickness (laminar): $\delta/\delta_t \approx Pr^{1/3}$. For gases ($Pr\approx0.7$) the two are about equal. For oils ($Pr\gg1$) the thermal layer is much thinner.
  • Unheated starting length $\xi$: the velocity boundary layer starts at $x=0$ but the thermal one starts at $x=\xi$. At a given $x$, the local $h$ is higher than with no starting length, because the thermal layer is thinner. Multiply $\bar h$ by the heated area only.
  • Uniform heat flux plates have $Nu_x$ 36% higher (laminar) or 4% higher (turbulent) than isothermal plates. Then $T_s(x) = T_\infty + q_s''/h_x$.
  • Cylinder in cross flow: local $Nu$ is highest at the forward stagnation point ($\theta=0$) and drops as the laminar boundary layer grows. It reaches a minimum near separation ($\theta\approx80^\circ$). At high $Re$, transition to turbulence causes a second rise before separation ($\theta\approx140^\circ$).
  • Sphere: as $Re\to0$, $\overline{Nu}_D \to 2$. That's pure conduction from a sphere into an infinite still medium.
  • Energy balances with radiation (roofs, workpieces in furnaces, thermocouples) are common. Convection and radiation can push in opposite directions, and the steady temperature sits between $T_\infty$ and $T_{sur}$.
  • The correlations are only good to about ±20%. Don't sweat the third significant figure.

Short Questions

S1 Laminar scaling

On a flat plate with laminar flow, the local coefficient at $x = 0.2$ m is 20 W/m²·K. What is the local $h$ at $x = 0.8$ m (still laminar)? What is $\bar h$ averaged from 0 to 0.8 m?

Show answer

$h_x \propto x^{-1/2}$, so $h(0.8) = 20\,(0.2/0.8)^{1/2} = 10$ W/m²·K. For laminar flow the average from the leading edge is twice the local value at the end.

$h_{0.8} = 10$ W/m²·K, $\bar h = 20$ W/m²·K

S2 Where does transition happen?

Air ($\nu = 1.6\times10^{-5}$ m²/s) flows at 12 m/s over a 1.5 m plate. Where does transition occur? Is this a mixed boundary layer problem?

Show answer

$x_c = Re_{x,c}\,\nu/u_\infty = (5\times10^5)(1.6\times10^{-5})/12 = 0.667$ m. Since $x_c \lt L$, yes. Use the mixed correlation $\overline{Nu}_L = (0.037Re_L^{4/5}-871)Pr^{1/3}$.

$x_c \approx 0.67$ m, so the boundary layer is mixed

S3 Turbulent average vs. local

If the boundary layer is turbulent from the leading edge, how does $\bar h_L$ compare with the local $h_L$ at the trailing edge?

Show answer

$h_x \propto x^{-1/5}$, so $\bar h_L = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^L h_x\,dx = \frac{5}{4}h_L$. You can also see it as the ratio $0.037/0.0296 = 1.25$.

$\bar h_L = 1.25\,h_L$

S4 Velocity effect

The free-stream velocity over a plate is doubled. By what factor does $\bar h$ change if the flow is (a) laminar everywhere, (b) turbulent everywhere?

Show answer

$\bar h \propto Re^{1/2}$ (laminar) or $Re^{4/5}$ (turbulent).

(a) $2^{0.5} = 1.41\times$   (b) $2^{0.8} = 1.74\times$

S5 Similarity scaling (like HW 6.29)

Tests on a bar in cross flow give $\bar h = 60$ W/m²·K at 10 m/s and $\bar h = 90$ W/m²·K at 20 m/s. Assume $\overline{Nu} = C\,Re^m Pr^n$. (a) Find $m$. (b) Estimate $\bar h$ for a geometrically similar bar twice as large at 20 m/s.

Show answer

(a) For the same bar and fluid, $\bar h \propto V^m$, so $m = \ln(90/60)/\ln(20/10) = 0.585$.

(b) $\bar h = \frac{k}{L}C\left(\frac{VL}{\nu}\right)^m Pr^n \propto V^m L^{m-1}$. At the same $V$, doubling $L$ gives $\bar h = 90\,(2)^{0.585-1} = 90(0.750)$.

$m = 0.585$; $\bar h \approx 67.5$ W/m²·K. Bigger objects have smaller $h$.

S6 Thermal vs. velocity boundary layer

For laminar flow of water ($Pr = 7$) over a plate, which is thicker, $\delta$ or $\delta_t$, and by roughly how much? What about a liquid metal ($Pr \ll 1$)?

Show answer

$\delta/\delta_t \approx Pr^{1/3} = 7^{1/3} = 1.91$. Momentum diffuses faster than heat in water. For liquid metals $Pr \ll 1$, so $\delta_t \gg \delta$.

Water: $\delta \approx 1.9\,\delta_t$. Liquid metal: thermal layer much thicker.

S7 Unheated starting length intuition

(a) At a fixed location $x$, does adding an unheated starting length raise or lower the local $h$? (b) Is the average $h$ over a heated strip of length $L-\xi$ higher or lower than for a plate of length $L-\xi$ heated from its own leading edge?

Show answer

(a) Higher. The thermal boundary layer only starts at $\xi$, so at $x$ it is thinner than it would be if heating started at 0, and the temperature gradient at the wall is steeper.

(b) Lower. Over the heated strip the velocity boundary layer is already thick (it started at $x=0$), so near-wall velocities are lower than on a fresh plate. Long problem L2 shows this with numbers.

S8 Which temperature for properties?

For each correlation, where do you evaluate properties: flat plate, Churchill–Bernstein, Hilpert, Zukauskas, Whitaker?

Show answer
Flat plate, Churchill–Bernstein, Hilpert: $T_f$. Zukauskas: $T_\infty$ with $Pr_s$ at $T_s$. Whitaker: $T_\infty$ with $\mu_s$ at $T_s$.

S9 Cylinder local Nusselt number

For a cylinder in cross flow at moderate $Re_D$ (laminar boundary layer), where on the surface is the local $Nu_\theta$ highest, and where is it lowest? What changes at very high $Re_D$?

Show answer

Highest at the forward stagnation point ($\theta = 0$), where the boundary layer is thinnest. It decreases as the layer grows and is lowest near separation ($\theta \approx 80^\circ$). Past separation it rises a bit from mixing in the wake.

At high $Re_D$ ($\gtrsim 10^5$), transition to turbulence near $\theta \approx 100^\circ$ produces a second maximum. Separation is delayed to about $140^\circ$, where a second minimum appears.

S10 Sphere limit

Why does $\overline{Nu}_D \to 2$ for a sphere as $Re_D \to 0$, while a flat plate's $Nu \to 0$?

Show answer

With no flow, a sphere still loses heat by steady conduction into the infinite surrounding fluid. The conduction resistance $1/(4\pi k r)$ gives $h = k/r = 2k/D$, so $Nu_D = 2$. An infinite plate has no such finite steady conduction solution.

S11 Steady-state energy balance with radiation

A small object hangs in a 900°C air stream inside a furnace whose walls are at 600°C. Write the steady-state energy balance. Where does the object's temperature end up?

Show answer

Convection in from the hotter air equals radiation out to the cooler walls:

$$\bar h A(T_\infty - T_s) = \varepsilon\sigma A\left(T_s^4 - T_{sur}^4\right)$$
$600^\circ\text{C} \lt T_s \lt 900^\circ\text{C}$. Solve iteratively, because $\bar h$ depends weakly on $T_s$ through $\mu_s$ and the radiation term is $T^4$.

S12 Turbulent plate: does length matter?

For a plate turbulent over its whole length, how does the total heat rate per unit width $q'$ scale with length $L$?

Show answer

$\bar h \propto L^{-1/5}$, so $q' = \bar h L\Delta T \propto L^{4/5}$. Doubling the length gives $2^{0.8} = 1.74\times$ the heat rate, not 2×.

L1 Mixed boundary layer on a flat plate like 7.20, 7.26

A flat plate 2 m long (in the flow direction) and 1 m wide is held at $T_s = 60^\circ$C. Air at 20°C and 1 atm flows over the top surface at 15 m/s.

  1. Where does transition occur?
  2. Find the average convection coefficient and the heat rate from the top surface.
  3. Find the local $h$ just before and just after transition, and at the trailing edge.
  4. A trip wire at the leading edge makes the flow turbulent over the whole plate. What is the new heat rate?
Show solution

Properties

Air at $T_f = (60+20)/2 = 40^\circ$C $= 313$ K: $\nu = 17.20\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.0273$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.705$.

(a) Transition

$Re_L = \dfrac{(15)(2)}{17.20\times10^{-6}} = 1.74\times10^6 \gt 5\times10^5$, so the boundary layer is mixed.

$x_c = \dfrac{(5\times10^5)(17.20\times10^{-6})}{15} = 0.573$ m

(b) Average coefficient and heat rate

$$\overline{Nu}_L = \left[0.037(1.74\times10^6)^{4/5} - 871\right](0.705)^{1/3} = 2467$$ $$\bar h = \frac{2467(0.0273)}{2} = 33.7\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K} \qquad q = \bar h A\,\Delta T = 33.7(2\times1)(40) = 2690\ \text{W}$$

(c) Local coefficients

Just before $x_c$ (laminar): $h = \dfrac{k}{x_c}0.332Re_{x,c}^{1/2}Pr^{1/3} = 9.95$ W/m²·K

Just after $x_c$ (turbulent): $h = \dfrac{k}{x_c}0.0296Re_{x,c}^{4/5}Pr^{1/3} = 45.5$ W/m²·K, more than a 4× jump.

Trailing edge: $h_L = \dfrac{k}{L}0.0296Re_L^{4/5}Pr^{1/3} = 35.4$ W/m²·K

(d) Fully turbulent

$$\overline{Nu}_L = 0.037Re_L^{4/5}Pr^{1/3} = 3242 \Rightarrow \bar h = 44.3\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K},\quad q = 3540\ \text{W}$$
$x_c = 0.57$ m; $\bar h = 33.7$ W/m²·K, $q \approx 2.69$ kW; $h$ jumps from 10 to 45 W/m²·K at transition; fully turbulent $q \approx 3.54$ kW (+31%)

Comment: HW problems like 7.20 and 7.26 tell you to assume turbulent flow over the whole surface. Then you use the 0.037 form with no −871 term.

L2 Unheated starting length like 7.41

Air at 25°C flows at 3 m/s over a plate 0.8 m long and 0.5 m wide. The first $\xi = 0.4$ m is unheated ($T_s = T_\infty$). The remaining 0.4 m is held at 75°C.

  1. Find the local $h$ at $x = 0.5$ m and at the trailing edge ($x = 0.8$ m). Compare with the values you'd get with no unheated length.
  2. Find the average $h$ over the heated section and the heat rate.
  3. Compare with a 0.4 m plate heated from its own leading edge.
Show solution

Properties

Air at $T_f = 50^\circ$C $= 323$ K: $\nu = 18.20\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.0280$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.704$.

$Re_L = 3(0.8)/18.20\times10^{-6} = 1.32\times10^5 \lt 5\times10^5$, so the flow is laminar everywhere. Note that $x$ is always measured from the leading edge of the plate.

(a) Local coefficients

At $x = 0.5$ m: $Re_x = 8.24\times10^4$, $Nu_x|_{\xi=0} = 0.332Re_x^{1/2}Pr^{1/3} = 84.8$, so $h|_{\xi=0} = 84.8(0.028)/0.5 = 4.75$ W/m²·K.

$$h_x = \frac{4.75}{\left[1-(0.4/0.5)^{3/4}\right]^{1/3}} = \frac{4.75}{(0.154)^{1/3}} = 8.86\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}$$

At $x = 0.8$ m: $Re_x = 1.32\times10^5$, $Nu_x|_{\xi=0} = 107.3$, $h|_{\xi=0} = 3.75$ W/m²·K, and $h_x = 3.75/[1-(0.5)^{3/4}]^{1/3} = 5.07$ W/m²·K.

(b) Average over heated section ($p = 2$)

$$\overline{Nu}_L|_{\xi=0} = 0.664Re_L^{1/2}Pr^{1/3} = 214.5$$ $$\overline{Nu}_L = 214.5\left(\frac{0.8}{0.4}\right)\left[1-(0.5)^{3/4}\right]^{2/3} = 235 \Rightarrow \bar h = \frac{235(0.028)}{0.8} = 8.22\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}$$

Use the heated area only: $q = \bar h (L-\xi)W\Delta T = 8.22(0.4)(0.5)(50) = 82$ W.

(c) Comparison

A 0.4 m plate heated from its own leading edge gives $\bar h = 10.6$ W/m²·K and $q = 106$ W.

$h(0.5) = 8.9$, $h(0.8) = 5.1$ W/m²·K (1.9× and 1.35× the no-$\xi$ values); $\bar h = 8.2$ W/m²·K, $q \approx 82$ W, about 23% less than a fresh 0.4 m plate

L3 Pipe in cross flow, then insulated like 7.68

A 40-mm-diameter pipe with surface temperature 70°C is exposed to a −10°C wind blowing across it at 8 m/s.

  1. Find the heat loss per unit length using Churchill–Bernstein.
  2. Repeat with the Hilpert correlation and compare.
  3. The pipe is wrapped in 15 mm of foam ($k = 0.026$ W/m·K). Assume the pipe wall stays at 70°C. Find the new heat loss and the outer surface temperature.
Show solution

Properties

Air at $T_f = (70 + (-10))/2 = 30^\circ$C $= 303$ K: $\nu = 16.19\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.0265$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.707$.

(a) Churchill–Bernstein

$Re_D = 8(0.04)/16.19\times10^{-6} = 19{,}770$

$$\overline{Nu}_D = 0.3 + \frac{0.62(19770)^{1/2}(0.707)^{1/3}}{[1+(0.4/0.707)^{2/3}]^{1/4}}\left[1+\left(\frac{19770}{282000}\right)^{5/8}\right]^{4/5} = 78.6$$ $$\bar h = \frac{78.6(0.0265)}{0.04} = 52.1\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K},\qquad q' = \bar h\pi D\,\Delta T = 52.1\pi(0.04)(80) = 524\ \text{W/m}$$

(b) Hilpert

For $Re_D$ between 4000 and 40,000: $C = 0.193$, $m = 0.618$. Then $\overline{Nu}_D = 0.193(19770)^{0.618}(0.707)^{1/3} = 77.7$, giving $\bar h = 51.5$ W/m²·K and $q' = 517$ W/m. That's within about 1%, well inside the ±20% uncertainty.

(c) Insulated

$D_o = 70$ mm. Conduction resistance: $R'_{ins} = \dfrac{\ln(70/40)}{2\pi(0.026)} = 3.43$ m·K/W.

The outer surface will be close to $T_\infty$, so evaluate properties at $T_f \approx 264$ K: $\nu = 12.70\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.0234$, $Pr = 0.716$. Then $Re_D = 8(0.07)/12.70\times10^{-6} = 44{,}100$, Churchill–Bernstein gives $\overline{Nu}_D = 127.6$, and $\bar h = 42.7$ W/m²·K, so $R'_{conv} = 1/(\bar h\pi D_o) = 0.107$ m·K/W.

$$q' = \frac{70-(-10)}{3.43 + 0.107} = 22.6\ \text{W/m},\qquad T_{s,o} = -10 + 22.6(0.107) = -7.6^\circ\text{C}$$

Check: $T_f = (-7.6-10)/2 = -8.8^\circ$C $\approx 264$ K. ✓

(a) 524 W/m; (b) 517 W/m; (c) 22.6 W/m (a 96% reduction), outer surface −7.6°C

Comment: once insulated, the foam resistance dominates (3.43 vs. 0.107). That's why the exact value of $h$ barely matters for (c).

L4 Thermocouple radiation error (sphere) like 7.79

A thermocouple bead (sphere, $D = 1.0$ mm, $\varepsilon = 0.6$) measures the temperature of air flowing at 5 m/s through a duct. The duct walls are at 400 K and the thermocouple reads 500 K.

  1. What is the true air temperature?
  2. Estimate the bead's time constant ($\rho = 8500$ kg/m³, $c = 400$ J/kg·K).
  3. Name two ways to reduce the measurement error.
Show solution

Energy balance on the bead (steady)

Convection from the hotter air equals radiation to the cooler walls:

$$\bar h(T_\infty - T_j) = \varepsilon\sigma(T_j^4 - T_w^4) \Rightarrow T_\infty = T_j + \frac{\varepsilon\sigma(T_j^4 - T_w^4)}{\bar h}$$

Properties (Whitaker uses $T_\infty$, which is unknown, so iterate)

Guessing $T_\infty \approx 520$ K gives 504 K after one pass. Re-evaluating at $T_\infty \approx 505$ K: $\nu = 39.4\times10^{-6}$ m²/s, $k = 0.041$ W/m·K, $Pr = 0.684$, $\mu = 271.7\times10^{-7}$ N·s/m². At $T_s = 500$ K, $\mu_s = 270.1\times10^{-7}$ N·s/m².

(a) True air temperature

$Re_D = 5(0.001)/39.4\times10^{-6} = 127$

$$\overline{Nu}_D = 2 + \left[0.4(127)^{1/2} + 0.06(127)^{2/3}\right](0.684)^{0.4}\left(\frac{271.7}{270.1}\right)^{1/4} = 7.18$$ $$\bar h = \frac{7.18(0.041)}{0.001} = 294\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}$$ $$\varepsilon\sigma(T_j^4 - T_w^4) = 0.6(5.67\times10^{-8})(500^4 - 400^4) = 1255\ \text{W/m}^2$$ $$T_\infty = 500 + \frac{1255}{294} = 504.3\ \text{K}$$

(b) Time constant

$\tau = \dfrac{\rho V c}{\bar h A_s} = \dfrac{\rho c D}{6\bar h} = \dfrac{8500(400)(0.001)}{6(294)} = 1.9$ s. Lumped capacitance is fine for a metal bead: $Bi = \bar h(D/6)/k \ll 0.1$.

(a) $T_\infty \approx 504$ K (the reading is about 4 K low); (b) $\tau \approx 1.9$ s; (c) use a radiation shield, a low-emissivity coating, a higher gas velocity, or a smaller bead (all of which raise $\bar h$ or cut radiation)

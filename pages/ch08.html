Ch 8: Internal Flow

Flow in tubes and ducts: mean temperature, energy balances, and the two classic surface conditions.

Jump to: ConceptsEquationsShort questionsLong problems

Key Concepts

  • Internal flow is confined by the walls, so the boundary layers eventually meet and the flow becomes fully developed. There's no free stream, so the reference temperature is the mean (bulk) temperature $T_m$. That's the temperature tied to the fluid's energy transport, $q = \dot m c_p\,\Delta T_m$.
  • Laminar or turbulent? Use $Re_D = \rho u_m D/\mu = 4\dot m/(\pi D\mu)$. Laminar if $Re_D \lt 2300$. Correlations for turbulent flow typically want $Re_D \gtrsim 10{,}000$ (Dittus–Boelter) or $\gtrsim 3000$ (Gnielinski).
  • Entry lengths: laminar $x_{fd,h} \approx 0.05Re_D D$ and $x_{fd,t} \approx 0.05Re_D Pr\,D$. For oils ($Pr \gg 1$) the thermal entry length can be longer than the tube. For turbulent flow both are short, about $10D$, so if $L/D \gt 10$ you can usually treat the tube as fully developed.
  • $h$ along the tube: $h$ is very large at the entrance and decays to the fully developed value. So the average $\overline{Nu}$ over a tube is $\geq$ the fully developed $Nu$.
  • Fully developed laminar flow: $Nu_D$ is a constant: 4.36 for uniform $q_s''$, 3.66 for uniform $T_s$. It doesn't depend on $Re$ or $Pr$. Then $h = Nu\,k/D$ depends only on $D$, not on the flow rate.
  • Uniform surface heat flux: $T_m$ rises linearly with $x$. In the fully developed region $T_s - T_m = q_s''/h$ is constant, so $T_s$ is parallel to $T_m$. The maximum wall temperature is at the outlet.
  • Uniform surface temperature: $T_s - T_m$ decays exponentially with $x$. That's why you use the log mean temperature difference, not the arithmetic mean.
  • External fluid instead of a fixed wall temperature: use the same exponential with $T_s \to T_\infty$ and $\bar h \to \bar U$ (or $1/R_{tot}$).
  • Non-uniform flux $q_s''(x)$: integrate $\dot m c_p\,dT_m = q_s''(x)\,P\,dx$ to get $T_m(x)$, then $T_s(x) = T_m(x) + q_s''(x)/h$. Find the maximum $T_s$ by setting $dT_s/dx = 0$.
  • Properties are evaluated at $\bar T_m = (T_{m,i}+T_{m,o})/2$. Since you often don't know $T_{m,o}$ yet, guess, solve, check, iterate.
  • Noncircular ducts: use the hydraulic diameter $D_h = 4A_c/P$. For an annulus, $D_h = D_o - D_i$.

Key Equations

Flow
$$Re_D = \frac{\rho u_m D}{\mu} = \frac{4\dot m}{\pi D\mu},\qquad \dot m = \rho u_m A_c,\qquad D_h = \frac{4A_c}{P}$$
Overall energy balance (any surface condition)
$$q = \dot m c_p (T_{m,o} - T_{m,i}),\qquad \frac{dT_m}{dx} = \frac{q_s''(x)P}{\dot m c_p}$$
Uniform surface heat flux
$$T_m(x) = T_{m,i} + \frac{q_s''P}{\dot m c_p}x,\qquad q = q_s''PL,\qquad T_s(x) = T_m(x) + \frac{q_s''}{h}$$
Uniform surface temperature
$$\frac{T_s - T_{m,o}}{T_s - T_{m,i}} = \exp\left(-\frac{PL\bar h}{\dot m c_p}\right),\qquad q = \bar h A_s \Delta T_{lm},\qquad \Delta T_{lm} = \frac{\Delta T_o - \Delta T_i}{\ln(\Delta T_o/\Delta T_i)}$$
External fluid at $T_\infty$
$$\frac{T_\infty - T_{m,o}}{T_\infty - T_{m,i}} = \exp\left(-\frac{\bar U A_s}{\dot m c_p}\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{\dot m c_p R_{tot}}\right),\qquad q = \frac{\Delta T_{lm}}{R_{tot}}$$

Short Questions

S1 Laminar or turbulent?

Water ($\mu = 855\times10^{-6}$ N·s/m²) flows at 0.03 kg/s through a 12 mm tube. Laminar or turbulent?

Show answer

$Re_D = \dfrac{4\dot m}{\pi D\mu} = \dfrac{4(0.03)}{\pi(0.012)(855\times10^{-6})} = 3720$

$Re_D \approx 3700 \gt 2300$: turbulent (low end, so Gnielinski fits better than Dittus–Boelter)

S2 Entry lengths

Water ($Pr = 6$) flows at $Re_D = 1000$ in a 10 mm tube. Estimate the hydrodynamic and thermal entry lengths.

Show answer

$x_{fd,h} = 0.05(1000)(0.01) = 0.5$ m and $x_{fd,t} = x_{fd,h}Pr = 3$ m.

0.5 m and 3 m. Unless the tube is much longer than 3 m, entry effects matter.

S3 Laminar: does flow rate matter?

In fully developed laminar flow with uniform $T_s$, what happens to $h$ if (a) the flow rate doubles, (b) the diameter is halved?

Show answer

$Nu_D = 3.66$ is fixed, so $h = 3.66k/D$.

(a) no change; (b) $h$ doubles

S4 Turbulent: does flow rate matter?

Same question for turbulent fully developed flow (Dittus–Boelter): what happens to $h$ if $\dot m$ doubles in the same tube?

Show answer

$h \propto Re_D^{0.8} \propto \dot m^{0.8}$.

$h$ increases by $2^{0.8} = 1.74\times$

S5 Sketch: uniform heat flux

Sketch $T_m(x)$ and $T_s(x)$ for uniform $q_s''$, including the entrance region. Where is the wall hottest?

Show answer

$T_m$ is a straight line. Near the inlet $h$ is very large, so $T_s$ starts close to $T_m$. The gap $T_s - T_m = q_s''/h$ grows through the entry region and becomes constant once fully developed, so the two lines are parallel there.

The wall is hottest at the outlet

S6 Why LMTD?

For uniform $T_s$, why is $q = \bar hA_s\Delta T_{lm}$ and not $\bar hA_s(T_s - \bar T_m)$ with the arithmetic mean?

Show answer

$\Delta T = T_s - T_m$ decays exponentially with $x$, not linearly. Integrating $\bar h P\Delta T\,dx$ over the length gives exactly the log mean. The arithmetic mean overestimates the average $\Delta T$, badly when $\Delta T_o \ll \Delta T_i$.

S7 Exponential approach

For a tube with uniform $T_s = 100^\circ$C and $T_{m,i} = 20^\circ$C, $\bar hPL/(\dot mc_p) = 0.5$. Find $T_{m,o}$. What if the tube is twice as long?

Show answer

$T_{m,o} = 100 - 80e^{-0.5} = 100 - 80(0.607) = 51.5^\circ$C. Twice as long: $100 - 80e^{-1} = 70.6^\circ$C.

51.5°C, then 70.6°C. Doubling length does not double the temperature rise.

S8 Hydraulic diameter

Find $D_h$ for (a) a 20 mm × 40 mm rectangular duct, (b) an annulus with $D_i = 25$ mm, $D_o = 50$ mm.

Show answer

(a) $D_h = 4(20\times40)/(2(20+40)) = 26.7$ mm. (b) $D_h = D_o - D_i = 25$ mm.

S9 Dittus–Boelter exponent

Engine coolant is cooled as it flows through a radiator tube. Which $n$ do you use in $Nu = 0.023Re^{0.8}Pr^n$?

Show answer
$n = 0.3$, because the fluid is being cooled ($T_s \lt T_m$). Use 0.4 for heating.

S10 Which equation for $q$?

Name the two equations that give the total heat rate for (a) uniform $q_s''$ and (b) uniform $T_s$.

Show answer

(a) $q = q_s''PL$ and $q = \dot mc_p(T_{m,o}-T_{m,i})$. (b) $q = \bar hA_s\Delta T_{lm}$ and $q = \dot mc_p(T_{m,o}-T_{m,i})$.

S11 Average vs. fully developed

Is the average $\overline{Nu}_D$ for a short tube larger or smaller than the fully developed $Nu_D$? Why?

Show answer

Larger. In the entry region the boundary layers are thin and $h$ is high, so averaging over the tube includes those high values.

S12 Hot gas in a duct, cold outside

Hot air flows through a thin metal duct with cold wind outside. Which equation gives the outlet temperature, and what goes in the exponent?

Show answer
$$\frac{T_\infty - T_{m,o}}{T_\infty - T_{m,i}} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{\dot mc_pR_{tot}}\right),\quad R_{tot} = \frac{1}{\bar h_i\pi D_iL} + R_{wall} + R_{ins} + \frac{1}{\bar h_o\pi D_oL}$$

L1 Electrically heated tube (uniform flux) like 8.18

Water at 0.2 kg/s is heated from 15°C to 45°C in a 25-mm-diameter tube 8 m long. A heater tape wrapped around the tube supplies a uniform heat flux.

  1. What heat flux is required?
  2. Is the flow turbulent? Can you assume fully developed conditions?
  3. Estimate the surface temperature at the outlet. Where is the wall hottest?
Show solution

Properties

Water at $\bar T_m = 30^\circ$C (303 K): $c_p = 4178$ J/kg·K, $\mu = 803\times10^{-6}$ N·s/m². At the outlet, $T_m = 45^\circ$C (318 K): $\mu = 599\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.638$ W/m·K, $Pr = 3.93$.

(a) Heat flux

$$q = \dot mc_p\Delta T_m = 0.2(4178)(30) = 25{,}070\ \text{W}$$ $$q_s'' = \frac{q}{\pi DL} = \frac{25070}{\pi(0.025)(8)} = 39{,}900\ \text{W/m}^2$$

(b) Flow regime

$Re_D = \dfrac{4(0.2)}{\pi(0.025)(803\times10^{-6})} = 12{,}700$, so turbulent. $L/D = 320 \gg 10$, so fully developed conditions are a good assumption over most of the tube.

(c) Outlet wall temperature

Local $h$ at the outlet uses properties at $T_{m,o}$: $Re_D = 4(0.2)/[\pi(0.025)(599\times10^{-6})] = 17{,}000$.

$$Nu_D = 0.023(17000)^{0.8}(3.93)^{0.4} = 96.4\ \ (n = 0.4,\ \text{heating}) \Rightarrow h = \frac{96.4(0.638)}{0.025} = 2460\ \text{W/m}^2\text{K}$$ $$T_{s,o} = T_{m,o} + \frac{q_s''}{h} = 45 + \frac{39900}{2460} = 61.2^\circ\text{C}$$
$q_s'' \approx 39.9$ kW/m²; $Re_D \approx 12{,}700$, turbulent and fully developed; $T_{s,o} \approx 61^\circ$C, and the wall is hottest at the outlet

Comment: Gnielinski gives $h = 2600$ W/m²·K and $T_{s,o} = 60.3^\circ$C, so the two agree within the correlations' uncertainty. A fully developed estimate at the inlet gives $T_s \approx 37^\circ$C, but the real inlet wall is cooler because $h$ is higher in the entry region.

L2 Constant wall temperature with property iteration like 8.19, 8.49

Water at 0.25 kg/s enters a 20-mm-diameter, 4-m-long tube at 20°C. Condensing vapor keeps the wall at 90°C. Find the water outlet temperature and the heat rate. Make sure your property temperature is consistent.

Show solution

Pass 1: guess $\bar T_m = 40^\circ$C (313 K)

$c_p = 4179$, $\mu = 657\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.632$, $Pr = 4.34$

$Re_D = \dfrac{4(0.25)}{\pi(0.02)(657\times10^{-6})} = 24{,}200$, turbulent. $L/D = 200$, so treat the flow as fully developed.

$Nu_D = 0.023(24200)^{0.8}(4.34)^{0.4} = 133$, giving $\bar h = 4205$ W/m²·K.

$$\frac{\bar hPL}{\dot mc_p} = \frac{4205\,\pi(0.02)(4)}{0.25(4179)} = 1.012 \Rightarrow T_{m,o} = 90 - 70e^{-1.012} = 64.6^\circ\text{C}$$

Pass 2: update $\bar T_m = (20+64.6)/2 \approx 42.6^\circ$C (316 K)

$\mu = 625\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.635$, $Pr = 4.11$. This gives $Re_D = 25{,}500$, $Nu_D = 135.5$, $\bar h = 4300$ W/m²·K, and an exponent of 1.035.

$$T_{m,o} = 90 - 70e^{-1.035} = 65.1^\circ\text{C},\qquad q = 0.25(4179)(65.1-20) = 47.2\ \text{kW}$$

Check with the LMTD: $\Delta T_{lm} = (70-24.9)/\ln(70/24.9) = 43.6$ K, so $q = \bar hA_s\Delta T_{lm} = 4300\pi(0.02)(4)(43.6) = 47.2$ kW ✓

$T_{m,o} \approx 65^\circ$C, $q \approx 47$ kW. The first guess was already within 0.5°C.

L3 Hot-air duct in a cross wind like 8.44

Hot air (0.15 kg/s, 400 K inlet) flows through a thin-walled, uninsulated metal duct ($D = 0.3$ m, $L = 20$ m). Outside air at 280 K blows across the duct at 5 m/s.

  1. Find the outlet temperature and the heat loss.
  2. What thickness of insulation ($k = 0.05$ W/m·K) keeps the outlet at 380 K or above? As a first estimate, neglect the effect of the insulation on the outside convection resistance.
Show solution

Inside coefficient

Air at $\bar T_m \approx 370$ K (after one iteration): $c_p = 1011$, $\mu = 216.9\times10^{-7}$, $k = 0.0315$, $Pr = 0.696$.

$Re_D = \dfrac{4(0.15)}{\pi(0.3)(216.9\times10^{-7})} = 29{,}400$, turbulent. $L/D = 67$.

$Nu_D = 0.023Re_D^{0.8}Pr^{0.3} = 77.4$ ($n = 0.3$ because the gas is cooling), giving $\bar h_i = 77.4(0.0315)/0.3 = 8.12$ W/m²·K.

Outside coefficient (cylinder in cross flow)

The duct surface averages about 308 K, so $T_f \approx 294$ K: $\nu = 15.34\times10^{-6}$, $k = 0.0258$, $Pr = 0.709$. $Re_D = 5(0.3)/15.34\times10^{-6} = 97{,}800$. Churchill–Bernstein gives $\overline{Nu}_D = 212$, so $\bar h_o = 18.2$ W/m²·K.

(a) Outlet temperature

For a thin wall, $U = (1/8.12 + 1/18.2)^{-1} = 5.62$ W/m²·K, so $UA = 5.62\pi(0.3)(20) = 106$ W/K. Also $\dot mc_p = 0.15(1011) = 151.7$ W/K.

$$T_{m,o} = 280 + (400-280)\exp(-106/151.7) = 280 + 120(0.498) = 339.7\ \text{K}$$ $$q = 151.7(400 - 339.7) = 9.15\ \text{kW}$$

(b) Insulation for $T_{m,o} \geq 380$ K

Required: $\dfrac{UA}{\dot mc_p} = \ln\dfrac{400-280}{380-280} = 0.182$. With $\dot m c_p = 151.9$ W/K at 390 K, $UA = 27.7$ W/K.

Per unit length, $R'_{tot} = L/UA = 20/27.7 = 0.722$ m·K/W. Then:

$R'_i = 1/(\bar h_i\pi D) = 0.129$, $R'_o = 1/(\bar h_o\pi D) = 0.058$, so $R'_{ins} = 0.722 - 0.129 - 0.058 = 0.535$ m·K/W.

$$\ln(D_o/D) = 2\pi k R'_{ins} = 2\pi(0.05)(0.535) = 0.168 \Rightarrow D_o = 1.183D = 0.355\ \text{m}$$
(a) $T_{m,o} \approx 340$ K, $q \approx 9.2$ kW; (b) $t = (D_o - D)/2 \approx 27$ mm

Comment: without insulation, the inside resistance (low-velocity gas, $h_i \approx 8$) is larger than the outside one. Both are small next to a few centimeters of insulation.

L4 Non-uniform heat flux: find the hottest point like 8.18b, 8.23

A tube of diameter $D$ and length $L$ is heated with a flux that tapers linearly to zero: $q_s''(x) = q_o''(1 - x/L)$. Fluid enters at $T_{m,i}$ with flow rate $\dot m$. Take $h$ as constant (fully developed).

  1. Derive $q$, $T_{m,o}$, $T_m(x)$ and $T_s(x)$.
  2. Find where the wall temperature is highest.
  3. Evaluate for $D = 30$ mm, $L = 3$ m, $q_o'' = 8000$ W/m², $\dot m = 0.04$ kg/s, $c_p = 4180$ J/kg·K, $h = 1200$ W/m²·K, $T_{m,i} = 20^\circ$C.
Show solution

(a) Derivation

$$q = \int_0^L q_s''\,\pi D\,dx = \pi Dq_o''\int_0^L\left(1-\frac{x}{L}\right)dx = \frac{\pi Dq_o''L}{2},\qquad T_{m,o} = T_{m,i} + \frac{q}{\dot mc_p}$$ $$\dot mc_p\frac{dT_m}{dx} = \pi Dq_o''\left(1-\frac{x}{L}\right) \Rightarrow T_m(x) = T_{m,i} + \frac{\pi Dq_o''}{\dot mc_p}\left(x - \frac{x^2}{2L}\right)$$ $$T_s(x) = T_m(x) + \frac{q_o''}{h}\left(1 - \frac{x}{L}\right)$$

(b) Hottest point

$$\frac{dT_s}{dx} = \frac{\pi Dq_o''}{\dot mc_p}\left(1-\frac{x}{L}\right) - \frac{q_o''}{hL} = 0 \Rightarrow x^* = L\left(1 - \frac{\dot mc_p}{\pi DhL}\right)$$

This is valid when $\dot mc_p/(\pi DhL) \lt 1$. Otherwise $T_s$ only decreases and the wall is hottest at $x = 0$.

(c) Numbers

$q = \pi(0.03)(8000)(3)/2 = 1131$ W. With $\dot mc_p = 167.2$ W/K, $T_{m,o} = 20 + 1131/167.2 = 26.8^\circ$C.

$\dot mc_p/(\pi DhL) = 167.2/[\pi(0.03)(1200)(3)] = 0.493$, so $x^* = 3(1 - 0.493) = 1.52$ m.

$T_m(1.52) = 25.1^\circ$C and $T_s(1.52) = 25.1 + (8000/1200)(1 - 0.507) = 28.4^\circ$C. For comparison, $T_s(0) = 26.7^\circ$C and $T_s(L) = T_{m,o} = 26.8^\circ$C.

$q = 1131$ W, $T_{m,o} = 26.8^\circ$C; the wall peaks at $x^* \approx 1.52$ m with $T_{s,max} \approx 28.4^\circ$C

Comment: the same steps (integrate the flux, add $q''/h$, differentiate) solve the sinusoidal case in HW 8.23.
